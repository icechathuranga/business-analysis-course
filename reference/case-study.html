The Homegrove case study

Homegrove is a fictional company created for this course. Any resemblance to a real business, product or person is coincidental, and every figure is illustrative. We use one case the whole way through so each technique builds on the last instead of starting cold.

The company

Homegrove is a mid-sized UK online retailer of homeware and lifestyle goods — about £42M revenue, 300 staff, roughly 180,000 orders a year, sold through its website and four shops. The warehouse is in Leicester; head office in Manchester.

How the BA gets involved

The COO emails IT: "Customers keep complaining about late deliveries — I think we need to rebuild the website. Can we scope a new site?"

That one sentence is the spark for the whole course. The COO has handed over a solution ("rebuild the website") before anyone has understood the problem. A business analyst who simply did as asked would spend a lot of money and very likely not fix anything. So the BA's first job is to separate symptom → problem → root cause.

The symptoms (what people first report)

  • Late-delivery complaints up about 60% year on year.
  • Repeat-purchase rate has fallen from 38% to 29% over 18 months.
  • Customer Service handles roughly 1,200 "where is my order?" contacts every week.
  • The site occasionally oversells — takes orders for items that aren't really in stock.

What the analysis uncovers

Working through strategy analysis, stakeholder interviews and process mapping, the BA finds the real cause. Orders do not flow automatically from the website to the warehouse. Twice a day a clerk exports orders to a spreadsheet and re-keys them into the warehouse system. That manual hand-off creates delays, keying errors, and no real-time view of stock — which is why the site oversells. The website itself is fine. The problem is a broken order-to-fulfilment process with no system integration.

The proposed change

An integrated Order Management System (OMS) that connects storefront ↔ warehouse ↔ couriers, with real-time stock and automatic order flow — so no one re-keys anything.

The numbers (used in the business-case module)

ItemFigure
One-off project cost£240,000
Annual operating cost£40,000
Gross annual benefit£300,000
Net annual benefit£260,000

The gross benefit is made of about £120k in fewer refunds and goodwill credits, £90k in labour saved by ending the re-keying, and £90k in extra margin from fewer lost sales and a small lift in repeat purchase.

The lesson Homegrove teaches, again and again: understand the problem before you choose the solution. Everything else in business analysis hangs off that.
