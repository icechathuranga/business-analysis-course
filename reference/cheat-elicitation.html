Cheat sheet — which elicitation technique when

Every technique below is one of the named BABOK® techniques [BABOK]. There is no single best one — choose by what you need, from whom, and under what constraints, and usually combine two or three to triangulate. (See Module 5.)

TechniqueBest forWatch out forEffort
Interviews [BABOK]Depth, nuance and sensitive topics; one or a few people who hold the knowledge or the politicsLeading questions that feed the interviewee your assumption; taking one view as factMedium (per person)
Workshops [BABOK]Building cross-functional agreement fast; resolving conflicting views by having people hear each otherWithout a facilitation plan the loudest voice wins and quieter experts go silentHigh
Survey or Questionnaire [BABOK]Breadth — input from a population too large to interview, such as customers or all agentsLeading or double-barrelled questions; no depth; low response ratesMedium (wide reach)
Observation [BABOK] (job-shadowing)Seeing how work really happens when it differs from how it is described — manual, habitual, workaround-heavy tasksThe observer effect — people work more carefully when watched; time-consumingHigh
Document Analysis [BABOK]Cheap current-state facts, rules and figures before you book a meetingDocuments go stale; the written process often differs from the real oneLow
Focus Groups [BABOK]Exploring why people feel as they do and letting views build on each otherA dominant participant sets the tone; they explain, they do not measureMedium
Prototyping [BABOK]Making vague ideas concrete; eliciting by reaction when people know it when they see itOver-polish makes people debate colours, or assume it is already builtMedium to high
Brainstorming [BABOK]Generating a range of options — causes, ideas, risks — before you narrowEarly judgement kills half-formed ideas; you must converge afterwardsLow
Interface Analysis [BABOK]The touchpoints where people, systems or parties exchange informationMapping only system-to-system interfaces and missing the human and external onesMedium

How to choose

Weigh four things: reach (how many people), depth (how rich the insight), cost and time, and the sensitivity of the topic. Politically charged subjects rule out a group setting — go one-to-one; a geographically spread workforce pushes you from workshops towards surveys and remote interviews; a regulated or manual process makes observation essential, because the gap between the documented and the actual procedure is exactly where the risk lives. No single source is trustworthy on its own, so combine methods and look for convergence. At Homegrove you pair observation (shadow Sam) with interviews (Dana, Marcus) and document analysis (the contact logs), then bring it together in a workshop — where three sources disagree, you have found either a risk or a requirement. Whatever you use, confirm what you heard before you build on it.
