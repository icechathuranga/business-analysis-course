Cheat sheet — requirement types & quality

The four requirement types

BABOK® classifies every requirement into one of four types. Keeping them straight stops the why, the what, and the build detail blurring together. (See Module 7.)

TypeWhat it isHomegrove example
BusinessA high-level need or outcome of the organisation (the why)Cut the late-delivery rate from 12% to under 4%, and weekly "where is my order?" contacts from 1,200 to under 400
StakeholderWhat a particular stakeholder or group needs (the bridge)Dana's team needs live order status on one screen; Sam's team needs orders to arrive without re-keying
Solution — functionalWhat the solution must do — a behaviour or featureThe system imports website orders automatically, with no re-keying
Solution — non-functional (NFR)A quality the solution must haveThe stock sync completes within 60 seconds so the site never sells stale stock (figure illustrative)
TransitionA temporary capability, needed only to move from the current state to the future one, then retiredMigrate open orders into the new Order Management System (OMS) and train warehouse staff

What makes a requirement good [industry]

Whatever the format, a good requirement is: clear and unambiguous, concise, complete, consistent with the others, correct, feasible, and — above all — testable. The quickest test of quality is: can you write a test for it? Replace every vague qualifier — "fast", "user-friendly", "robust" — with a number or a concrete, checkable condition. "Delivery tracking should be fast" fails; "the customer is emailed within 15 minutes of any delivery-status change" passes.

INVEST — a checklist for user stories [industry]

  • Independent — it does not depend on other stories to deliver value
  • Negotiable — it is a placeholder for a conversation, not a fixed contract
  • Valuable — it delivers something a user or the business values
  • Estimable — the team can size it
  • Small — it fits comfortably in one iteration (if not, it is an epic — decompose it)
  • Testable — you can write acceptance criteria that prove it is done

Acceptance criteria: Given / When / Then [industry]

Acceptance criteria state when a requirement is done. The Gherkin format writes them as Given [context], When [event], Then [outcome], covering the main path and the important variations. They become the test conditions at UAT (User Acceptance Testing). The Homegrove headline story and its criteria:

As a customer, I want to see live delivery status for my order, so that I stop having to phone to ask where it is.

Given a dispatched order, when the customer opens "My Orders", then the current courier status and estimated delivery date are shown; and when status changes, the customer is notified by email within 15 minutes.

That single testable "within 15 minutes" is what makes the story buildable and checkable, and ties it straight to cutting the 1,200 weekly contacts.
