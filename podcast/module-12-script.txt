Welcome back. In the last episode we looked at the integrated system after it had already gone live, and asked whether it really worked. In this one we step back in time a little, to how a system like that gets built in the first place, because the way you choose to build it changes your whole job as an analyst.

Here is the choice at the heart of it. Our fictional retailer, Homegrove, could build its new Order Management System, or OMS, the joined-up system connecting website, warehouse and couriers, in one big release, specified completely up front, then constructed to the plan. Or it could build it in small, working slices: shipping the most valuable piece first, learning from it, then deciding what to build next. For a system with this much uncertainty, the second way is almost always wiser. Ship live delivery status first, watch whether those where-is-my-order calls actually fall, and then choose what comes next.

The two ends of that spectrum have names you will have met before. A predictive approach plans and specifies most of the requirements up front: plan first, then build. An adaptive approach delivers in short iterations, keeps the requirements deliberately light until just before each piece is built, and re-plans constantly. And it genuinely is a spectrum, not a switch. In real life you will almost always be somewhere in the middle, in a hybrid. At Homegrove, the deep integration and the payment handling want careful, predictive specification; the customer-facing delivery-tracking features are far better built adaptively, shipped and improved bit by bit. A fixed courier-contract deadline gives you the predictive frame, and the features flow adaptively inside it. One warning: do not call a project agile while still insisting every requirement is signed off before anyone builds a thing. That is simply predictive work wearing agile clothes.

I am the business analyst, or BA. Let me introduce the team around me on an adaptive project. The most common frame is called Scrum: work organised into short, fixed iterations, with a product owner, or PO, who owns what gets built and why; someone who protects the process; and the developers who build. There is also Kanban, where there are no fixed iterations at all. Work simply flows across a board, pulled into the next stage only when there is capacity to handle it, with deliberate limits on how much is in progress at once, so the team never starts more than it can finish.

That raises the question every new agile analyst asks: what is the difference between the product owner and me? The product owner is the one person accountable for the value of the product, with the final say on the order of the work. The analyst does the analysis that makes those decisions sound: eliciting needs, modelling the process and the data, writing clear stories and acceptance criteria, exposing the dependencies and the risks. At Homegrove, Dana, who lives the customer pain every single day, is a natural product owner, with you supplying the rigour behind her calls. The trap to avoid is an analyst quietly making priority decisions that were never theirs to make.

Let me give you three practical tools that will quickly feel like second nature. The first is user story mapping. You lay the user's journey out left to right as a backbone of activities, then hang the detailed stories beneath each one, most essential at the top. For Homegrove the backbone is the customer's order journey: browse, order, pay, fulfil, deliver, and return. Mapping it together, out loud, reveals gaps that a flat list of features always hides, and it makes a lovely question visible to everyone: what is the smallest slice we could ship that still helps the customer all the way across that journey? The one thing people forget is the unhappy path, returns, failed payments, missing stock, which at Homegrove is exactly where the pain lives.

The second tool is keeping the backlog healthy, which we call refinement. One ordered list. You re-order it by value and risk as you learn, you detail and size the few items at the top so the next iteration is ready, and you deliberately leave the items further down coarse and rough, because they will change, and polishing them now is simply waste.

The third tool is splitting stories, and there is a right way and a wrong way. The right way is to split vertically, into thin slices, each of which delivers something real that a user can actually see and use. Homegrove's delivery-status story splits beautifully along its own acceptance criteria: one story for showing the courier status and the estimated date, a second for sending the customer that email within fifteen minutes of a change. Each slice is independently valuable and independently testable. There is a handy checklist for a good story, remembered by the word invest, which asks that each one be independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, and testable. The wrong way is to split horizontally, by technical layer, build the database, then the connection, then the screen, because not one of those is something a customer can use, so not one can be shown or judged.

Two shared checklists then keep the quality honest. A Definition of Ready says when a story is fit to be pulled into an iteration: clear, small enough, with its acceptance criteria written and no blocking unknowns. A Definition of Done says when it is truly finished: built, tested, meeting its criteria, and genuinely shippable. At Homegrove, the delivery-status story is ready only once those two criteria are written and the courier-feed question is answered, and it is done only once both criteria pass their test and that email really does fire within fifteen minutes. A Definition of Done that stops at code written is how half-tested work gets called finished, and then comes back later as defects.

And underpinning all of it is a single mindset: just in time, and just enough. You analyse each item in just enough detail, just before it is built, so your understanding is as fresh as it can possibly be, and nothing is wasted on items that end up dropped. You do not write every story up front. At Homegrove you detail delivery status now, because it is next; the returns stories stay one-line placeholders until they rise up the list. But do hear the warning buried in this. Just enough must never quietly slide into not enough, because agile means less waste, not less rigour.

So, to carry away. Choose predictive or adaptive by how much you genuinely know up front. Let the product owner own priority while you supply the analysis. Slice the work vertically into small, shippable pieces. And do your thinking just in time and just enough, because that is agile analysis, and it is a different discipline, not a lazier one.
