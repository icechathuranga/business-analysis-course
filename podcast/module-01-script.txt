Welcome back. Last time we looked at what a business analyst does. In this episode we zoom right out, because before you can judge whether any change is a good idea, you have to understand the business it is meant to serve — what it is for, how it makes money, and where it is trying to go. This is the work the standard calls strategy analysis, and it has two halves: understanding the current state, and defining the future state. A BA — a business analyst — who skips this ends up lovingly polishing one corner of a business that may be heading somewhere else entirely.

Think of this as a set of lenses. Each one shows you something a little different, and the real art is picking the right lens for the question in front of you, rather than running every framework as a ritual and producing a thick pack that nobody reads.

The first lens is the business model canvas. It is a one-page picture of how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value, laid out in nine linked blocks — who your customers are, what you offer them, how you reach them, how you keep your relationship with them, how you make your money, and then the resources, the activities, the partners and the costs behind all of that. The trick is not filling in nine tidy lists; it is spotting the connections between them. For Homegrove — our fictional teaching retailer — one block jumps straight out: the key activity is fulfilment and delivery, which is precisely where the pain is being felt. And a weakness in delivery quietly poisons the whole promise of convenient, good-value homeware. That is the canvas earning its keep.

The next lens I will mention more briefly. It is an alignment check that runs top to bottom through a business, from its vision, down to its mission, then its objectives, then its strategy, and finally the day-to-day tactics. You write each level down, and then you read upward, asking: does each tactic actually serve a strategy, each strategy an objective, and so on? Any orphan is a flag. At Homegrove the stated direction is growth through customer retention, and the strategy is to make the whole buying-and-receiving experience dependable. Now read the proposed tactic — rebuild the website — upward against that, and it does not obviously serve dependable receiving. That little mismatch is a clue worth holding on to.

Now the lens almost everyone has heard of: SWOT. That stands for strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats — the first two internal to the business, the last two out in the wider world. Most people list a few adjectives in each box and stop there, and a SWOT that stops there is just wallpaper. The real work is crossing the boxes: how could a strength seize an opportunity, and which weakness, left alone, lets a threat bite? At Homegrove the telling cross is this — a weakness, unreliable delivery, is eroding a real strength, a loyal customer base. Repeat custom has already slipped from thirty-eight per cent down to twenty-nine per cent over eighteen months. That one sentence, crossing a weakness with a threat, tells you more than four tidy lists ever could.

Two further lenses look outward, beyond the business itself. One is PESTLE, which scans the forces acting on a company from outside — political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental. For Homegrove: squeezed household budgets make customers far less forgiving of a late order; shoppers now expect parcel tracking as standard; and couriers and retailers routinely plug their systems into one another automatically. The other outward lens is Porter's five forces, which looks at the competitive pressure in an industry — rivalry, the threat of new entrants, substitutes, and the bargaining power of buyers and suppliers. The reading for Homegrove is sharp: buyer power is high, because customers switch retailer easily after just one bad delivery. And in a market where buyers hold the power, reliability is a weapon — so losing it is strategically dangerous.

The last lens is the balanced scorecard, which tracks performance across four connected views — financial, customer, internal process, and learning and growth — so that a business does not chase short-term money while quietly starving the things that sustain it. Laid out this way, Homegrove's fulfilment trouble is visibly the lever behind a customer outcome, repeat purchase, and a financial one, revenue retained — not some backroom detail to be shrugged off.

Now, here is where all of this leads, and it is the point of the whole episode. These lenses feed three linked outputs. The first is a clear business need — the problem or opportunity, stated at the level of the whole business, that any change must address. For Homegrove the context points consistently to one place: the business is chasing growth through retention, and something about how customers receive their orders is undermining it. Notice that the need still says nothing at all about a website or a system. The second output is the drivers — the pressures that make change worth doing now: the rising complaints, the slipping repeat-purchase rate, the mounting customer-service load. Naming them turns it would be nice to improve into here is why we must act. And the third output is the desired future state, described deliberately as outcomes and capabilities, never as a chosen solution: customers receive their orders on time and in full, can see where an order is, and are never sold something that is not actually in stock. No platform named. That restraint is the entire discipline.

For the practitioners listening, the mature skill here is sequencing and evidence. Choose your frameworks by the question they answer — the canvas to see the whole, the outward scans for the environment, SWOT to synthesise it all into a decision, the alignment check to test that tactics really serve goals. In practice a tight canvas, plus a properly crossed SWOT, plus that alignment check, often does more than all six run half-heartedly. And keep current state and future state as genuinely separate tasks. Most of these tools describe where you are; the discipline is to resist leaping to the future until the current state is evidenced — and, when you do describe the future, to describe it as capabilities and outcomes, not as a named system. The moment you write the future state is a new platform, strategy analysis has quietly collapsed back into jumping to a solution.

So we leave this episode holding a business need, and not a single solution named. In the next episode, we take that business-level need and sharpen it into a precise, evidence-backed problem — and finally begin to uncover what is really going wrong at Homegrove.
