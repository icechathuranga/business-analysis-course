Welcome to Module eight. Here is the situation at Homegrove. You have done the analysis, and now you have a long and tempting wish-list: real-time stock, automatic order import, delivery tracking, a returns workflow, loyalty integration, international shipping, and more besides. But Priya, the chief operating officer and the project's sponsor, wants two things at once: fewer complaints and tight cost control. You simply cannot build everything at the same time. So two jobs fall to you. First, prioritisation, deciding what the first release fixes. And second, keeping that decision honest as scope, people, and pressures shift over time. Get this wrong and you either gold-plate something nobody asked for, or, worse, you quietly drop the one requirement that actually ends the retyping. This is the heart of today's episode.

Let me give you the golden rule of prioritisation before anything else: you must prioritise against a fixed boundary, a deadline or a budget. Priorities only mean something when there is a line you cannot cross. Say everything is important, and you have said nothing at all.

The friendliest method, and the one to reach for first, has a memorable name: MoSCoW. The capital letters stand for Must have, Should have, Could have, and Won't have this time. A Must is something the release genuinely has no value without. A Won't is not "never," it is simply "not in this release," and writing it down visibly turns dropped scope into a decision rather than a disappearance. At Homegrove, the Musts are real-time stock sync, automatic order import with no more retyping, and delivery status visible to the customer. The Shoulds are courier integration, connecting to the courier's application programming interface, or API, and a returns workflow. The Coulds are self-service rescheduling and a loyalty scheme. And the Won'ts, this time around, are international shipping and third-party marketplaces. Now look closely at those three Musts, because there is a lovely lesson in them. They map exactly onto the root cause, the broken manual hand-off, plus the one symptom customers actually feel, the lack of delivery visibility. Not a word about rebuilding the website. The big warning with MoSCoW is this: if nine out of ten items on your list end up as a Must, you have not prioritised, you have just relabelled the backlog. Challenge the Musts hard, and keep them a minority of the effort.

Once things are sorted into bands, you often need to sequence within a band, and a simple grid does it: value against effort. Plot each item by how much business value it brings and how much effort it takes. Up pop four zones: quick wins, big bets, fill-ins, and money pits. At Homegrove, delivery-status visibility lands as a glorious quick win, high value to anxious customers, modest effort, because the Order Management System, the OMS, already holds the data. Courier integration, by contrast, is a big bet, real value, but it depends on an outside party. One trap to sidestep: do not mistake a cheap feature for a quick win if it secretly depends on an expensive thing being built first.

For a larger backlog, there is a more numeric method worth knowing, with a slightly intimidating name: Weighted Shortest Job First. The idea behind it is elegant. You rank each item by its cost of delay divided by its job size. In plain terms, how much it hurts to wait, set against how big the job is. That division rewards the small, urgent, high-value items. And when you run it at Homegrove, something instructive happens. Delivery-status visibility jumps right to the top, precisely because it is small, a useful cross-check on your MoSCoW list. Reassuringly, the three Musts still rank above the Shoulds. A word of caution, though. These numbers are a relative ranking tool, not a precision instrument. Arguing whether a score is a seven or an eight just wastes the room.

There is one more method that is less about the maths and more about buy-in, and it is called the hundred-dollar method, also known as cumulative voting. You give each stakeholder a hundred points to spend across the requirements, however they like, and the totals reveal the collective priority. The real insight is often hiding in the spread. At Homegrove, when Dana from customer service, Marcus from fulfilment, and Tom from marketing each spend their hundred points, Marcus quietly funds the back-office plumbing, the stock sync and the order import, while Dana and Tom pour their points into what the customer actually sees. Neither of them is wrong. And your job is not simply to average the votes, but to surface the shared objective sitting underneath those very different bets. Forcing a fixed budget like this is exactly what makes the trade-offs real, because nobody, however senior, can give everything a hundred.

Now to the other half of this module, the part that is less glamorous but quietly decides whether projects succeed: managing requirements over time. The backbone here is a single distinction, the baseline versus the current state. The baseline is your signed-off, agreed snapshot. Everything before approval is negotiable. Everything after it is a change request that must earn its way in. And that is how you survive the great silent killer of good projects: scope creep.

Let me show you change control with a very human Homegrove moment. Midway through the project, Tom from marketing pops over, full of enthusiasm, and asks to add loyalty-scheme integration now, not later. The amateur move is to say "yes, of course" in the corridor. The professional move is to log it, and run an impact analysis: what else does this touch, and what does it cost? And the analysis is clear. Adding it now would delay the Musts and push out the payback, which lands at around eleven months. So the change board defers it to a later release. Not a flat "no," a documented "not yet." That one discipline, saying yes only by decision and never by drift, protects everything you have worked for.

Two more tools hold this together. The first is traceability, linking each requirement upward to the business objective it serves and downward to the design and the tests that prove it. So delivery-status visibility traces up to the objective of cutting those weekly contacts from twelve hundred to under four hundred, and down to the test cases that will check it. The second is approval, and here is an exam favourite worth remembering. Formally approving requirements is a recorded governance gate. Silence is not sign-off. If no one with the authority has explicitly said yes, you are building at risk.

A closing thought from experienced practitioners. Prioritisation is a negotiation, not a calculation. All of these methods, MoSCoW, value versus effort, the numeric ones, are devices for structuring a conversation and making trade-offs visible. Their output is an input to a decision, never the decision itself. And each has its own failure mode worth knowing: MoSCoW tempts everyone to inflate the Musts, the value-and-effort grid can hide dependencies, and the numeric methods invite false precision. So read the room as much as the scores.

So that is prioritising and managing requirements: always against a fixed boundary, matching the method to the moment, and holding a firm baseline so that change is controlled rather than left to drift. You have now chosen what to build first. The very next question any sponsor will ask is whether it is all worth the money, which takes us to Module nine, where we build the options, test their feasibility, and write the business case.
