Welcome back. This is, if I am honest with you, the episode the whole course is built around. Because the most valuable thing a business analyst — a BA — ever does is make sure the business solves the right problem. And to do that, you need to keep four little words crisply apart, because in practice they get muddled constantly. Those words are symptom, problem, need, and root cause.

Let me define them with our teaching company, Homegrove, in mind. A symptom is what people notice — deliveries are late, customers keep phoning. A problem is the business-level thing going wrong behind those symptoms — we cannot reliably fulfil orders on time and in full. A business need is the outcome that must be true whatever the solution turns out to be — orders fulfilled on time, in full, with accurate stock. And a root cause is the underlying reason the problem exists in the first place — and, crucially, it is found, not assumed.

Now recall Homegrove's spark. The chief operating officer emailed to say, I think we need to rebuild the website, to stop the late-delivery complaints. There is a name for that move. It is called solutioneering — jumping to a solution before the problem has even been framed. Rebuild the website answers a question nobody has actually asked. And here is the uncomfortable truth we are about to uncover together: the website was never the problem. Scope and build a shiny new site, and Homegrove would spend heavily while the deliveries stayed just as late. The BA's job is to slow that leap down, just enough to ask: what is actually going wrong, and why?

So how do we find the why? The gentlest and most powerful tool is almost childishly simple. It is called the five whys — you simply keep asking why, down a chain, until you reach something you can actually act on. Let me walk it with you, because this is the beating heart of the episode.

Why are Homegrove's deliveries late? Because orders are reaching the warehouse late. And why are orders reaching the warehouse late? Because there is no automatic flow of orders from the website through to the warehouse. Why not? Because the website and the warehouse systems are simply not connected to each other. And why does that matter so very much? Because — and here is the thing the analysis finally lays bare — twice a day, a warehouse clerk exports the orders into a spreadsheet and re-keys them, by hand, into the warehouse system. Twice a day. By hand.

Sit with that for a moment, because everything else falls out of it. Orders wait for the next batch, so they are already late before anyone has picked a single item. Fingers slip during the re-keying, so some orders go out wrong. And because stock is only updated in those two daily batches, the website has no live view of what is really on the shelves, so it cheerfully sells things that are already gone — what the trade calls overselling. One broken, manual hand-off, quietly causing late deliveries, wrong orders, and oversells, all at once. The storefront, meanwhile, is working perfectly well. The real problem was never the website; it is a broken order-to-fulfilment process, with no connection between the systems.

That is the reveal. Now let me show you how a BA pins it down, so it survives contact with a sceptical boardroom. The core tool is the problem statement — a short, disciplined sentence that names who is affected, what is going wrong, and the impact, with no solution smuggled inside it. For Homegrove it reads roughly like this: for our online customers, who expect their orders on time and in full, the problem is that orders are frequently late, sometimes wrong, and occasionally accepted for items that are out of stock — which is driving late-delivery complaints up by around sixty per cent year on year, repeat purchases down from thirty-eight per cent to twenty-nine per cent, and about twelve hundred where-is-my-order contacts every single week. Not one word about websites. Then you audit it: strike out any word that names a solution. If what remains still reads as a problem, it is clean.

There are several other tools that support this work, and I will name them lightly, so you simply know they exist. A context diagram draws a boundary around the process you are studying, so the scope cannot quietly sprawl. An issue tree breaks a big, daunting question into smaller parts, with no overlaps and no gaps, so your analysis does not miss a cause. A rich picture is a deliberately messy sketch of a human situation, feelings and politics very much included, because a tidy flowchart would hide exactly the tension that matters most. And a jobs-to-be-done statement keeps you honest about the customer's real goal. Here is Homegrove's: when I order homeware online, I want it to arrive when promised and to know where it is, so I can plan my day and stop chasing it. Notice what the customer never hired Homegrove to do — redesign its website.

Finally, you turn the framed problem into concrete targets. You lay the current state beside the future state and you name the gap. Homegrove's late-delivery rate, for instance, runs at around twelve per cent today, and the target is under four per cent — a gap of roughly eight percentage points. And you write those targets as SMART objectives — that is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. So: the late-delivery rate from twelve per cent down to under four; those weekly where-is-my-order contacts from about twelve hundred down to under four hundred; repeat purchase from twenty-nine per cent back up to thirty-three; oversell incidents from around twenty-five a month to under two. Every one has a baseline and a target — and the date on each is agreed with the sponsor, never simply guessed.

A thought for the practitioners. Root cause is a decision, not a destination — the five whys can run on forever, and the skill is knowing where to stop: at the deepest cause the business can actually act on and afford, not the philosophically deepest one. At Homegrove you could keep asking why, past the systems not being connected, but the actionable cause is that broken, manual hand-off. Stop there, and note the rest as context. And remember that framing is political — telling a chief operating officer that their website rebuild misses the point is a delicate business. You do it not by contradicting them, but by showing the chain: symptom, to evidenced problem, to tested root cause, to the gap. When the problem statement names no solution and the data points squarely at the hand-off, rebuild the website simply fails to survive its own analysis — and you never once had to say the words, you are wrong.

So Homegrove's real problem turns out to be a broken, manual order-to-fulfilment process — not the storefront at all. In the next episode, we turn to the people: how to find every stakeholder, judge who truly matters, and win over the ones who would rather you just went away.
