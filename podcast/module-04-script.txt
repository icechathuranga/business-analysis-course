Welcome back. By now, at Homegrove — our fictional teaching retailer — you have framed the real problem and mapped the people. So the chief operating officer, Priya Shah, quite reasonably expects a plan. How will you run the analysis? How long will it take? Who signs off what? And how on earth will you keep hundreds of facts and requirements straight across the warehouse, customer service, technology and finance? A business analyst — a BA — who elicits brilliantly but cannot plan the work will lose the room. Planning is what turns trust me into here is how.

This whole episode lives in one part of the standard: the part where the BA plans their own work, rather than planning the product. And it really answers five questions, before you dive in. How will we work? Who will we involve? How are decisions made? How will we handle the information? And how will we know whether we did a good job? Let us take them one at a time.

First, how will we work — the approach. The big choice is between a predictive approach, where you plan most of the detail up front, in formal documents, waterfall-style; and an adaptive approach, where you discover and deliver in small increments, agile-style, just in time. Most real engagements sit somewhere on the line between the two. At Homegrove, the likely solution — an integrated order management system that moves stock and order data between systems and talks to the external couriers — is a lovely example of why. The integration and data pieces are risky, and they want a careful, more predictive touch. But the customer-facing delivery-tracking features can be built adaptively, released and improved bit by bit. So you plan a sensible hybrid, and you say so out loud, so that nobody expects a two-hundred-page specification for a feature that is far better learned by shipping it. The trap to avoid is choosing an approach by fashion — we are agile now — rather than by the context actually in front of you.

Second, who will we involve. You did the real substance of this last episode — the register, the power and interest grid, and the responsibility matrix, which sorts people into who is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed. The point to carry forward here is simply that planning the engagement is part of this planning work, while the ongoing collaboration happens later, as the project runs. Your plan might say Priya gets a crisp steer every fortnight, Sam the warehouse supervisor is involved early as the process expert, and Nadia in finance is pulled in before the business case, not after.

Third, how are decisions made — and this is governance. Governance is the agreed answer to who decides, who approves, and how changes are assessed once things have been settled. Settle it early, because the first time two stakeholders disagree is the very worst moment to discover that nobody ever agreed who decides. At Homegrove you agree that Marcus, the head of fulfilment, approves the future-process requirements; Nadia signs off the business case; and any scope change above an agreed size goes back up to Priya. A simple log tracks every open issue, risk, action and decision, each with a named owner — so the open question of whether the couriers' systems can hand over real-time tracking sits on that log, with Lee, in technology, named against it.

Fourth, how will we handle the information. This is the plan for where requirements are captured, how detailed they are, and how they are traced, stored and reused. The key idea is traceability. At Homegrove you decide that requirements will be numbered and traced all the way from Priya's business goals right down to individual user stories — so that when someone asks, months later, why are we building live delivery tracking, you can trace it straight back to those twelve hundred weekly where-is-my-order contacts it is meant to cut. The alternative — requirements scattered across emails, chats and three different spreadsheets — means nobody can ever answer what did we agree, and why.

Fifth, and the one most often skipped: how will we know whether we did a good job? This has two parts. One is estimation — forecasting how much effort your work will take. A lovely, honest method is three-point estimation. For each activity you give three figures: an optimistic one, a most-likely one, and a pessimistic one. Then you take a weighted average — you add the optimistic figure, plus four times the most-likely figure, plus the pessimistic figure, and you divide the whole lot by six. That weighting stops a single hopeful guess from running away with your plan. For Homegrove's discovery phase you might add up stakeholder analysis, the interviews, the process mapping, writing it all up, and the review cycles, to something like a hundred and twenty-four hours of effort before contingency. And I will stress that those numbers are purely illustrative — they are there to show the method, not real figures for the case. The cardinal sin is giving a single confident number, with no range and no stated assumptions, so that the first little surprise makes you look wrong, rather than simply uncertain — which, at the start, you always are.

The other part is measuring your own practice, not just the solution's success. You might track how often a signed-off requirement later has to be reworked. At Homegrove, suppose you notice that rework is running high on the returns workflow. You hold a short lessons-learned session, realise you never actually shadowed the returns desk, and adjust the plan before that gap costs you any more. That is a BA getting better at the craft in real time, which is the whole point of the exercise.

A closing nuance for the practitioners. Plenty of analysts plan the product in loving detail and quite forget to plan the analysis — so the same facts get elicited twice, because nobody decided how the information would be structured and reused. Others write a governance plan with no teeth — a named approver who is never actually asked to approve, so scope creeps in silently. And many never measure their own performance at all — lots of metrics on the solution, none on their own rework or their stakeholders' satisfaction — so the very same mistakes quietly repeat on the next project. Remember, a plan is not a document you file and forget; it is a living thing you review against reality.

So that is planning: choose your approach by context, settle who decides before the first disagreement, keep every requirement traceable back to a goal, estimate with honest ranges, and measure your own work as well as the solution's.
