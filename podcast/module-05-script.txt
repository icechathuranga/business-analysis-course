Welcome to Module five. This is the episode about elicitation, the craft of getting the real needs out of people. And that word, elicit, is chosen with great care, because you do not simply collect requirements the way you might gather fallen leaves in the garden. People almost never hand you a clean, correct need. They give you symptoms. They leap straight to solutions. They forget the awkward exceptions, and they cheerfully contradict one another. So the job of the business analyst, or BA, is to draw the truth out, patiently and deliberately, in each person's own world.

Let me set the scene with the company we follow all the way through this course: Homegrove. Homegrove is a mid-sized online homeware retailer here in the United Kingdom, with revenue of around forty-two million pounds and about three hundred staff. And Homegrove has a problem. The chief operating officer, the COO, is already convinced the answer is to rebuild the website. Meanwhile, twice a day, a warehouse supervisor named Sam Reilly exports orders into a spreadsheet and retypes them, by hand, into the warehouse system. And over in customer service, Dana Okoro's team fields around twelve hundred "where is my order?" contacts every single week. Three people, three very different pictures of the same problem. Elicitation is how you get the real story out of all of them.

Here is the rhythm that carries every elicitation activity, whatever the technique. Three beats: prepare, conduct, confirm. Prepare means decide your goal, choose your method, line up the right people, and ready your materials. Conduct means run the session well and capture what you learn. And confirm means play back what you heard, so the stakeholder can correct it before it ever hardens into a requirement. Skip the preparing and you waste a senior person's precious hour. Skip the confirming and a simple misunderstanding quietly turns into something somebody builds. That last beat, confirming, is the cheap step beginners always skip, and it is the one that saves you.

Two skills run quietly through all three beats. The first is active listening. That means giving your full attention, reflecting back what you heard, noticing what is not being said, and resisting that very human urge to fill a silence. The second is a good interview guide, a prepared, ordered set of open questions, with room to follow an interesting thread wherever it leads. Both of those live in the course's elicitation plan template, so you are never starting from a blank page.

Now, the heart of this module: choosing the right technique. And here is the liberating truth. There is no single best technique. There is only the best fit for what you need, from whom, and under what constraints. Use interviews when you want depth from one or two people. Use workshops when you need a room full of people to reach agreement quickly. Use observation when the work in real life differs from how people describe it. Use document analysis to learn the current state cheaply, before you ever book a meeting. Use a survey to reach a large population, and a focus group to understand why people feel as they do. The real skill is weighing reach, depth, cost, and the sensitivity of the topic, and then usually combining two or three methods, so they corroborate one another.

Let me show you that in action, because this is where Homegrove comes alive. Picture yourself shadowing Sam through one full retyping cycle. You already knew, on paper, that orders get exported to a spreadsheet and typed into the warehouse system twice a day. But watching it is a different thing entirely. You time it. You see the typos creep in. And you catch the exact, painful moment when an order that lands just after a batch has run simply sits there, waiting, before the warehouse even knows it exists. That single observed detail is pure gold for the design that comes later. It also teaches you something about observation itself. People tend to work more carefully when they are being watched, so you stay long enough for normal habits to reassert themselves, and you cross-check against the contact logs.

Then you interview Dana. Your guide moves gently from the open and easy, "talk me through a typical Monday on the contact lines," toward the specific, "when a customer rings to ask where their order is, what can you actually see on your screen?" And out comes the finding that changes everything: her agents have no live view of dispatch at all. There is your justification for customer-facing delivery tracking, straight from the person who feels the pain every day. Notice what you must never do here. Never ask a leading question like "so it is the website that is the problem, isn't it?" Feed people your assumption and they will hand it straight back to you, and you will mistake your own echo for a finding.

Finally you bring everyone together in a workshop, a facilitated session with a clear objective, a neutral facilitator, and someone to take notes. You run a future-state workshop with Marcus from fulfilment, with Dana, with Sam, and with Lee Tan from the technology team, to design how order-to-fulfilment should work with no more retyping. And something lovely happens. Having Sam and Lee in the same room for ten minutes surfaces an integration constraint that emails had been hiding for weeks. That is the quiet magic of a workshop. People hear each other.

A few more techniques belong in your kit. Document analysis is how you turned "we get loads of complaints" into hard evidence, roughly twelve hundred contacts a week, around twenty-five overselling incidents a month. Interface analysis maps the points where systems and teams exchange information, and at Homegrove it names the villain precisely: between the storefront and the warehouse there is no automated connection at all, just Sam and a spreadsheet. Naming that missing link is half the solution. And prototyping lets you sketch a rough "My Orders" screen and let customers react to it, because people who struggle to describe what they want will very often know it the moment they see it.

Whatever technique you use, finish with that third beat: confirm. Send the interview write-up back. Circulate the workshop decisions. Play the observed process back to Sam. A stakeholder correcting a summary email costs you minutes. The very same mistake, discovered later in testing, costs a fortune.

And one last piece of seasoned advice: triangulate, always. No single source is trustworthy on its own. People misremember, documents go stale, and behaviour changes under the spotlight. So weigh what Dana says, against what the logs record, against what you see with your own eyes at the desk. Where all three agree, you have real confidence. Where they disagree, you have found either a risk or a requirement, and either way, you have found something worth knowing.

So that is elicitation: drawing needs out rather than collecting them, moving through prepare, conduct and confirm, and choosing your method to fit the question in front of you. Once you have gathered all that truth, your next job is to make it visible, and that is exactly where we are going in Module six, where we turn Homegrove's tangled order process into clear pictures everyone can read.
