Welcome back. In the last episode we worked out what was really going wrong at Homegrove — our fictional teaching retailer — and traced it to a broken order-handling process rather than the website. But here is a hard truth: being right is not enough. A correct analysis can die quietly in a meeting if you have not brought the right people along with you. So this episode is about stakeholders, because stakeholder work is how a business analyst, a BA, makes good analysis actually land. We will cover how to find your stakeholders, how to judge who matters and why, and how to plan to engage every one of them — including the ones who would rather you simply went away.

Let us start with a definition. A stakeholder is anyone affected by the change, anyone who can affect it, or anyone who holds information you need. And you do three things with them, in that order: you identify them, you analyse them, and then you plan to engage them.

Identifying them is more than jotting down the obvious names. A lovely prompt for this is the onion diagram — picture the solution sitting at the centre, with rings radiating outward: first the people who operate it, then the wider organisation around them, then the external parties out at the very edge. It is that outer ring that reminds you the courier companies exist at all. For Homegrove, the list comes alive with real people. There is Priya Shah, the chief operating officer, who is sponsoring the project. Marcus Doyle, the head of fulfilment, who owns the broken process. Dana Okoro, who leads customer service and feels the daily pain. Sam Reilly, the warehouse supervisor, who actually does the twice-daily re-keying by hand. Lee Tan, the information technology manager. Nadia Haddad in finance, who will sign off the money. And Tom Vance, who heads marketing and cares deeply about customers coming back. A quiet warning here: list real humans, not just job titles, and never, ever forget the low-power people who happen to hold the knowledge you most need.

Once you have your list, you analyse it, and the classic tool is a power and interest grid — sometimes called the Mendelow grid. You plot each person on two axes: how much power they have over the change, and how much interest they have in it. The four corners suggest four strategies. High power and high interest — manage them closely. High power but low interest — keep them satisfied. Low power but high interest — keep them informed. And low on both — simply monitor. At Homegrove, Priya and Marcus sit firmly in manage closely. Lee and Nadia hold high power but more moderate interest, so you keep them satisfied. And here is the subtlety: Sam, the warehouse supervisor, scores low on formal power — but you deliberately involve him early anyway, because he knows the current process better than any living soul. The grid is a prompt, not a verdict. And do re-plot it as things move: the moment the business case needs a signature, Nadia's interest leaps right up.

The third tool answers a question that causes more trouble than almost any other: who actually decides this? The tool is RACI, which stands for responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed. For each activity, you name exactly who is responsible — who does the work; who is accountable — who owns the outcome, and there is only ever one accountable person per activity; who is consulted for their input; and who is merely informed of the result. At Homegrove, when it comes to producing the business case, you, the BA, are responsible for writing it, but Nadia in finance is accountable, because she is the one who signs it off. Marcus is accountable for the fulfilment requirements, because he owns the process. The golden rule is this: every activity has one, and only one, accountable owner. Two accountable people is not shared ownership — it is a decision with no owner, and it will stall.

Now, sometimes a stakeholder is not a single person but a whole population — customers, say, or warehouse pickers. For those, you build a persona: a short, named sketch of a typical human, with their goal, their day, and the specific frustration this change should remove. Homegrove has Rushed Rita, thirty-four, who orders homeware on her phone between meetings, wants to know where her parcel is without having to telephone anyone, and today ends up ringing customer service because the site shows her nothing at all after dispatch. Rita speaks for the twelve hundred or so where-is-my-order contacts the team fields every single week. Just be sure to ground your personas in real evidence, not imagination, or they merely encode your own assumptions.

And now the part people find hardest: resistance. I want to reframe it for you completely. Resistance is information, not misconduct. Remember Sam, who does that twice-daily re-keying by hand. Sam quietly fears that a new system means he is no longer needed — and honestly, that is an entirely rational fear. The case shows the manual hand-off ties up roughly three full-time jobs' worth of effort, some of which will be redeployed. So you do not promise Sam that nothing will change, because you cannot, and he would know a hollow promise when he heard one. Instead, you involve him early as the expert on the current process, you are honest that roles will shift, and you let him help shape what comes next. Handled with genuine respect, a resister very often becomes your strongest advocate. The real mistake — and even experienced analysts make it — is going silent with a resister because the conversation feels uncomfortable. Silence just confirms their worst fear and hardens the opposition.

One framing point for the practitioners. The standard splits this work across two homes, and it is worth keeping them straight. Planning how you will engage people — the list, the grid, the approach — is a planning task. The ongoing, day-to-day work of keeping those people engaged once the project is running is a collaboration task. Same people, two different moments: you plan first, then you collaborate throughout. And treat your stakeholder register as a living, slightly political document — keep your candid notes on who really holds influence in your own working copy, and share a neutral engagement plan with the room.

So, to sum up. Find your stakeholders with a register and an onion diagram. Analyse them with a power and interest grid and a responsibility matrix. And treat resistance, like Sam's, as information to be understood rather than an obstacle to be bulldozed.
