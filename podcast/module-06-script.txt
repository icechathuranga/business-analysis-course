Welcome to Module six. Last time, in Module five, you did the hard, human work of drawing out what people really need. Now comes a quieter kind of power: turning all of that tangled, invisible way of working into clear pictures, pictures that every single stakeholder can read, challenge, and agree on. That is modelling, and it is the subject of today's episode.

Here is why it matters, and Homegrove shows it beautifully. Homegrove, you will remember, is our mid-sized online homeware retailer. And everyone there knew how the order process worked, except that no two people described it the same way. Priya Shah, the chief operating officer, or COO, blamed the website. Marcus Doyle, who runs fulfilment, blamed the warehouse. Dana's customer service team just knew the phones never stopped ringing. Three honest people, three different stories. So what do you do? You draw the order process on a single page. And the moment you do, the real culprit steps into the light.

A model does three jobs. It exposes what is really happening. It gives everyone one shared picture instead of a tangle of words. And it turns a fuzzy need into something precise enough to build and to test. The techniques fall into three families: models of the process, models of behaviour, and models of the data. Let us walk through the ones that matter most.

Start with the process, and with the single most useful diagram a beginner can learn: the swimlane map. Imagine a set of horizontal lanes, one for each role or system. Every step sits in the lane of whoever actually performs it. And here is the clever part. Every time an arrow crosses from one lane into another, that is a hand-off, and hand-offs are where the trouble hides.

So you draw Homegrove's order-to-fulfilment just as it is today, with lanes for the customer, the website, the warehouse team, the warehouse system, the courier, and customer service. The customer places an order and pays. The website records it. And then the flow has to cross into the warehouse team's lane, where, twice a day, Sam's team exports the new orders into a spreadsheet and retypes each one into the warehouse system. Two hand-offs, right there: orders in, retyped orders out. And that narrow gap is where the delay lives, where the keying errors creep in, and where the lack of real-time stock begins. One picture, and the whole root cause is suddenly impossible to ignore.

But, and this is the part beginners skip, a beautiful map that just sits on the wall changes nothing. You have to follow it with the question: so what? That is the difference between process modelling, which draws the picture, and process analysis, which interrogates it. You walk each step and ask: does this add value? Why does work wait here? Where do the errors get in? At Homegrove, the analysis flags the export-and-retype steps as pure, non-value-adding delay, and it names the missing real-time stock link as the cause of the overselling.

This is also the moment to meet a lovely pairing: as-is and to-be. The as-is is how work happens today. The to-be is how it should work after the change. And the gap between those two pictures, that gap is literally your list of things to build. At Homegrove, the as-is runs: website, then the twice-daily spreadsheet retype, then the warehouse, then the courier, with no status ever coming back. The to-be runs: website, then automatic order import into a new Order Management System, an OMS, then real-time stock, then the warehouse, then a courier link, then live delivery status for the customer. The difference between those two pictures is the system requirements. One warning, though. Never draw the to-be before the as-is is honest, or you will design elegant solutions to problems you never actually understood. That, in a nutshell, is the "just rebuild the website" trap.

Let me give you one more process lens, because it reframes the whole complaint. It is called value stream mapping, and it separates the time that genuinely adds value, the real work a customer would happily pay for, from the time that is simply waiting. At Homegrove, picking and packing an order takes mere minutes of real work. But an order placed just after a batch run waits for the next retype before the warehouse even sees it. The wait utterly dwarfs the work. And that tells you something crucial: do not speed up your pickers. Remove the wait.

Now the second family, behaviour. Here you might sketch a user journey map, which follows the customer's experience and, importantly, how they feel at each stage. Homegrove's journey goes: browse, order, wait, and then nothing. No visibility. Anxiety. A phone call to customer service. And eventually, at last, the parcel. That blind waiting stage, with no information at all, is the low point of the whole journey, and it is precisely what generates those twelve hundred weekly "where is my order?" contacts. Live status aims straight at that low point. A related tool is the use case, which describes, step by step, how a person uses the system to reach a goal. And the real craft of a use case is never writing only the happy path. The extensions, what happens when there is no courier update, when payment fails, when an order splits, are where the real requirements hide.

And the third family, data. This sounds dry, but do stay with me, because it is where overselling finally dies. A simple data model captures the things the business cares about and how they relate. One customer places many orders. One order has many order lines. And here is the key one: one product has a stock level per location. That little phrase, stock level per location, is what makes real-time stock possible, and real-time stock is what stops Homegrove selling things it does not actually have. There is also a gentler cousin called concept modelling, which simply pins down what words mean, because until everyone agrees that an oversell means accepting an order for stock that is not really there, they cannot even discuss the problem precisely.

A word of seasoned advice to carry with you. Choose the model that fits your question, not the one you happen to enjoy drawing. A swimlane answers "who does what, and where are the hand-offs?" A value stream answers "where does the time go?" A data model answers "what do we store, and how does it relate?" Reaching for your favourite notation regardless of the question is the commonest habit worth breaking. And keep each diagram at a single level of detail, a high-level sketch for your sponsor, a detailed one for the builders, never the two muddled together on one page.

So that is modelling: making the invisible visible, modelling the as-is honestly before you design the to-be, and always, always asking "so what?" after you draw. These clear pictures you have built do not just hang on a wall. They feed straight into the words you will write next, and that is where we are headed in Module seven, where we learn to specify and write requirements a developer can build and a tester can check.
