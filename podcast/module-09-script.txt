Welcome to Module nine, and in many ways this is the episode everything has been building towards. Because at Homegrove, the analysis is done. You know the root cause is the manual order hand-off, not the website. You have a prioritised list of what to build first. But here is the thing: nobody has spent a single penny yet. The business case is where all that careful analysis finally becomes a decision, where Priya, the chief operating officer, and Nadia, the finance business partner who signs off the spend, decide whether to actually fund the new system. A weak business case wastes all the good work that came before it. A strong one wins the money. So let us learn to write one that wins.

The very first discipline is this: never present a single option. A one-option business case is not analysis, it is an advertisement, and decision-makers see straight through it. So you frame a spread of realistic ways to meet the need, and you always include the do-nothing option as your baseline, because that is the thing every other choice has to beat. At Homegrove there are four. Option zero: do nothing. Option one: rebuild the website, the chief operating officer's original instinct. Option two: buy an off-the-shelf Order Management System, an OMS, and integrate it, connecting storefront, warehouse, and couriers, with real-time stock and automatic order flow. And option three: build a custom system in-house. Now watch what happens to the first two. Do nothing, and rebuild the website: neither one touches the twice-daily retyping behind the late deliveries and the overselling. So neither fixes the root cause, and they fail the screen immediately.

That screening matters, and there is a technique that makes it rigorous: decision analysis. You score each surviving option against weighted criteria, things like fixing the root cause, total cost, speed to benefit, delivery risk, and future flexibility. But here is the subtle trap, and it is a lovely teaching point. When you score them, do-nothing comes out deceptively well, because it is cheap, it is fast, and it is low-risk. On a simple average, it looks almost respectable. So you must treat fixing the root cause as a knockout criterion, a go, no-go gate that an option has to pass before its average even counts. Apply that gate, and options zero and one are out, no matter how tidy their numbers look. Buy-and-integrate is the clear winner.

Before you trust any of that, though, you check that each surviving option is genuinely doable, and that is feasibility analysis. Can the technology actually do it? Will the business adopt it? Does the money work, can you hit the timeline, and are there any data-protection or contractual snags? At Homegrove, the buy-and-integrate option is technically sound and economically strong, though operationally sensitive, because Sam the warehouse supervisor understandably fears for his job. And because option two means buying from a supplier, you also run a vendor assessment, judging the candidates not on the sticker price but on how well they fit the Musts, their total cost of ownership, their support, how they handle your data, and whether there is a clean exit clause. The cheap licence with a punishing renewal is a classic trap. Just hold the boundary firmly in mind: feasibility only screens out the non-starters. It is the business case that actually decides.

And now the part Nadia in finance is really waiting for: the money. Three staple measures, and I will walk you through each with Homegrove's actual figures.

Start with the costs and the benefits. There is a one-off project cost, the software, the integration, the rollout, of two hundred and forty thousand pounds. Then there is an ongoing operating cost, licence and support, of forty thousand pounds a year. Against that sit the benefits, which add up to a gross annual benefit of three hundred thousand pounds a year. That three hundred thousand breaks into three parts: about a hundred and twenty thousand pounds from fewer refunds and goodwill credits on late or wrong orders; about ninety thousand pounds of labour saved by ending the twice-daily retyping, which is roughly three full-time roles, partly redeployed rather than simply cut; and about ninety thousand pounds of extra margin from fewer lost sales and a modest lift in repeat custom. Now here is the figure to hold on to. Take the three hundred thousand pounds of gross benefit, subtract the forty thousand pounds of annual running cost, and you are left with a net annual benefit of two hundred and sixty thousand pounds a year. That net figure is what the measures are built on.

First measure: simple payback. How long until the project pays for itself? You divide the one-off cost, two hundred and forty thousand pounds, by the net annual benefit, two hundred and sixty thousand pounds a year, and you get about eleven months. In under a year, Homegrove has earned back what it spent.

Second measure: return on investment, or ROI. Over three years, the net benefit adds up to three times two hundred and sixty thousand pounds. Set the original outlay against that, and the three-year return on investment comes to two hundred and twenty-five per cent. Put plainly, over three years the project returns more than three pounds for every single pound invested.

Third, and the most complete: net present value, or NPV. This one respects a simple truth, that a pound in three years' time is worth less than a pound today, so it discounts future money back into today's terms. Using a ten per cent discount rate, the three-year net present value of this project is about four hundred and six thousand pounds. And because that figure is comfortably positive, the project clears its hurdle, it genuinely creates value. One habit to keep you honest: never quote a net present value without naming the discount rate, and never over-claim the benefits. That ninety-thousand-pound labour saving assumes the staff are redeployed, not cut, so say so plainly, or finance will quietly discount it.

Of course, numbers alone do not win a case; you also have to show you have thought about what could go wrong. So you build a short risk register. The courier integration might slip, so you keep it a Should, not a Must, and you engage the technology team and the partners early. Sam the warehouse supervisor fears for his job, so you redeploy rather than cut, and you involve him early. Data might go wrong at the switch-over, so you cleanse it, and you run the old and new systems in parallel before you make the switch. And then you set out the change strategy itself, not the destination, but the journey. At Homegrove that is a phased rollout: pilot the new system on a single warehouse shift, run it alongside the old spreadsheet process to prove the automatic import really works, then switch over fully. Cost the journey, not just the destination.

And here is the single most important rule for the document itself: lead with the recommendation. Busy executives read from the top down, so put the ask right at the front. For Homegrove it is wonderfully clear. Proceed with option two, the buy-and-integrate system. It is the only feasible option that fixes the root cause. It pays back in about eleven months. It returns two hundred and twenty-five per cent over three years. And it has a three-year net present value of about four hundred and six thousand pounds. Bury that recommendation on page nine, and your case will read as weaker than the analysis behind it truly deserves.

So that is the business case: always frame several options including do-nothing, screen with feasibility, decide with weighted scoring and knockout criteria, quote payback, return on investment and net present value together, and lead with your recommendation. You have now built a case that is ready to win the money. The last step is actually to win it, and that is Module ten, where we communicate, present, and secure sign-off.
