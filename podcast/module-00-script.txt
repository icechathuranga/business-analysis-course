Welcome back. In this episode we are going to answer a question that sounds simple but trips a lot of people up: what does a business analyst really do? By the end you will be able to explain the role in plain words, say how it differs from the roles that sit around it, and see the shape of the whole course laid out, so that everything we cover later has somewhere to hang.

Let me give you the one-sentence version first. A business analyst — a BA — works out what a business needs and why, then defines what any solution must do to meet that need, clearly enough that the people who build it, the people who test it, and the people who pay for it all share one and the same understanding. The real value in that is risk reduction. The most expensive risk in any change is building the wrong thing well, and the BA exists to shrink it.

Day to day, that means talking to people, reading how the business truly works rather than how the organisation chart says it works, mapping out processes, writing needs down as requirements, helping choose between options, and then checking afterwards that the change actually helped. You will learn each of those skills as the course unfolds.

Here is a mental picture I would like you to keep. Imagine two groups who rarely speak the same language. On one side, the business — people who feel a problem but describe it in outcomes and frustrations. On the other, delivery — the developers, testers and suppliers who need precise, buildable detail. The BA sits in the gap between them and translates both ways. That two-way-translator position is why BAs spend so much of their time listening, asking, and writing things down plainly.

Now, the BA is often confused with three neighbouring roles, and the quickest way to tell them apart is to ask which question each one owns. The business analyst owns the question: what problem are we solving, and what must the solution do? A project manager owns a different question: how do we deliver this on time, on budget, with these people? A product owner — a role you meet on agile teams — owns the question: what is the most valuable thing to build next? And a systems analyst owns: how will the system actually be built to do this? One person sometimes wears two of these hats on a small team, but the questions stay distinct. Confuse them, and you end up with a beautifully managed schedule for building the wrong thing.

If you remember only one idea from this entire course, let it be this: understand the problem before you choose a solution. Think back to Homegrove — our fictional teaching retailer — whose chief operating officer emailed to say, I think we need to rebuild the website, to stop the late-delivery complaints. That email names a solution without ever stating the problem. The BA's instinct is to pause, gently, and ask: what is actually going wrong for customers, and why? That pause is not obstruction. It is the single most valuable thing a BA does. We will meet this pattern again and again, and one whole module is built around breaking it.

What can Homegrove see so far? Only symptoms. Late-delivery complaints climbing sharply. Repeat custom slipping away. A customer-service team swamped with where-is-my-order calls. What the business has not yet done is find out why. That gap — between the symptoms everyone feels and the cause nobody has yet named — is exactly where your work as a BA will begin.

Let me now hand you the map of the profession, so later modules have a frame. The body of knowledge we align to, the business analysis body of knowledge, or BABOK, organises the work into six knowledge areas. You do not need to memorise them — just let them wash over you. The first is planning and monitoring your own analysis work. The second is elicitation and collaboration — drawing needs out of people and keeping them engaged. The third is requirements life cycle management — tracing, prioritising and approving requirements over time. The fourth is strategy analysis — understanding where the business is now, where it wants to be, and the change in between. The fifth is a bit of a mouthful: requirements analysis and design definition — the modelling and specifying work. And the sixth is solution evaluation — checking that whatever got delivered actually delivers value.

Around those six, the standard adds a set of underlying competencies — the personal skills a good BA leans on, such as analytical thinking and communication — and a handful of perspectives that show how the work flexes in different settings, like agile or business intelligence. It also catalogues around fifty techniques — interviews, process modelling, prioritisation, and so on. Those techniques are the practical tools you will spend most of this course actually learning.

One more distinction to tuck away. There are two broad approaches to change. A predictive approach plans the work up front and delivers in larger, planned stages — the plan-first, waterfall style. An adaptive approach works in small increments and expects to learn and adjust as it goes — the agile, iterative style. The BA mindset does not change between them; only the timing and size of the work does. A strong BA works comfortably in either, and most real projects turn out to be a blend of the two.

A quick word for those of you already in the field. Two nuances worth holding on to. First, those six knowledge areas are not a sequence. Beginners read them top to bottom and imagine a tidy waterfall, but in reality they interleave constantly — you will be managing requirements while still eliciting, and revisiting strategy when a new constraint appears halfway through. Treat the map as a checklist of concerns always in play, not a route march. Second, role boundaries are negotiated, not fixed. That clean table of the BA, the project manager and the rest is a teaching device; on real teams the lines blur, and the most common failure is not ignorance of the boundaries but a silent assumption about who owns a decision. A senior BA names that ambiguity early — who approves scope changes here, you or me? — rather than discovering it in the middle of a dispute.

So, to recap. A BA works out what the business needs and why, then defines what a solution must do, and in doing so reduces the risk of building the wrong thing. The BA is a two-way translator between the business and delivery. Each neighbouring role owns a different question, and confusing them wastes effort. And above all, understand the problem before you choose a solution.
