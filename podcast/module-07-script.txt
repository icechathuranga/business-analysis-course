Welcome to Module seven. By now, with Homegrove, you have framed the problem, you have heard the real needs, and you have modelled the process and the data. But here is the sobering truth: none of that gets built until it is written down in a way a developer can act on and a tester can check. Write "make deliveries better" and you will get something, just not what Homegrove needs. Write, instead, "the customer can see live courier status and an estimated delivery date, and is emailed within fifteen minutes of any status change," and now you have something buildable, testable, and tied directly to cutting those twelve hundred weekly "where is my order?" calls. Good requirements are the bridge between understanding and delivery, and building that bridge is today's craft.

Let me start with a tidy idea that prevents a world of confusion: requirements come in four types, and keeping them straight matters enormously. Business requirements are the high-level outcomes the organisation wants, the why. Stakeholder requirements are what a particular person or team needs, the bridge in the middle. Solution requirements are what the solution must actually do, and they split in two: functional, meaning the behaviour and features, and non-functional, meaning the qualities, like speed and security. And transition requirements are the temporary things you need only to get from the old world to the new, like migrating data and training staff, which then fall away. At Homegrove, the business requirement is to cut the late-delivery rate from twelve per cent to under four, and the weekly contacts from twelve hundred to under four hundred. A stakeholder requirement is that Dana's team needs live order status on one screen. A functional solution requirement is that the system imports website orders automatically. And a transition requirement is migrating the open orders into the new Order Management System, the OMS, and training the warehouse staff. Needed once, then gone.

Now, whatever format you write in, a good requirement has one quality above all others: it must be testable. If you cannot imagine writing a test for it, it is not a requirement yet, it is a wish. And the great enemy of testability is the vague qualifier. Fast. User-friendly. Robust. They feel like requirements, but you cannot test an adjective. So the discipline is simple and ruthless: replace every vague word with a number or a concrete, checkable condition. "Delivery tracking should be fast" tells a developer nothing. "The customer is emailed within fifteen minutes of any delivery-status change," that, you can test. For requirements written as user stories, there is a handy checklist with the memorable name INVEST, where each letter is a quality: independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, and, there it is again, testable.

That brings us to the star of modern requirements writing: the user story. A user story is deliberately short. As a role, I want a capability, so that I get a benefit. Homegrove's headline story reads: as a customer, I want to see live delivery status for my order, so that I stop having to phone to ask where it is. But, and this is where beginners go wrong, the little story card is not the whole requirement. It is the promise of a conversation. The real rigour lives in the acceptance criteria, which state exactly when the story is done. A popular way to write them is called Gherkin, and it follows a given, when, then shape. For our story: given a dispatched order, when the customer opens "My Orders," then the current courier status and estimated delivery date are shown; and when the status changes, the customer is notified by email within fifteen minutes. That one testable phrase, within fifteen minutes, is what turns a nice idea into something a team can actually build and a tester can actually verify.

There is one more move you will reach for constantly, and it is called functional decomposition. It simply means breaking a requirement that is too big to be clear into smaller, testable pieces. At Homegrove, order management is far too large to build, or even to estimate, as one single lump, so you break it down: import orders, sync stock, manage dispatch, show delivery status, handle returns. And show delivery status breaks down again, into the customer's view, the email notification, and the courier feed. You keep going until each piece is small enough to specify and estimate, and then, crucially, you stop. Decomposing forever, chasing the very last conceivable atom of detail, is its own kind of waste.

Here is a technique that quietly prevents disasters: business rules analysis. A business rule is a policy, stated on its own, independent of any system, something like "we never accept an order for stock we do not have." Why pull the rules out and keep them in a list of their own? Because rules change, and when one does, you want to edit a single line, not go hunting through a dozen diagrams. At Homegrove, the rule that ends overselling is this: an order may only be accepted if the real-time available stock is greater than or equal to the quantity ordered. Enforce that one rule, and the overselling simply stops.

And when a decision has several conditions, prose will betray you every time. All those "if, unless, except when" sentences always hide a missing case. The cure is a decision table, where you lay out every combination as its own row. Take Homegrove's decision to accept or reject an order. Is there enough stock? Is the payment authorised? That gives four combinations. And the one that matters most is the row where there is payment but not enough stock. That is the overselling case, and in a table it simply cannot be forgotten. You reject that line and offer a backorder. Laid out as a grid, the dangerous case stares right back at you.

Now, the requirements that sink projects: the non-functional ones, the qualities. Stakeholders will happily describe features all day, but they almost never volunteer that the stock sync must complete within, say, sixty seconds, or that the link between storefront and system must be available for virtually all of trading hours, or that customer and payment data must be protected in line with data-protection law. Those particular figures are illustrative, agreed with Lee in the technology team, but the principle is iron: every quality needs a measurable target, not an adjective. "Must be secure" and "must be fast" are not requirements until they carry a number. These are the ones most often forgotten, and the most expensive to bolt on late.

Finally, two checks that sound alike but are gloriously different: verify and validate. To verify a requirement is to ask, "is this well written?" Clear, complete, consistent, testable. That is building the thing right. To validate it is to ask, "is this the right requirement?" Does it actually serve the business goal? That is building the right thing. You can write a requirement to flawless perfection and still be building entirely the wrong thing. At Homegrove, verifying the delivery-status story confirms its criteria are unambiguous; validating it confirms it genuinely serves the goal of cutting those twelve hundred weekly contacts, which it does, so it stays. Do both. And keep them firmly distinct.

So that is specifying requirements: classify them by type, make every one testable by trading vague words for numbers, choose the right format, a story for a capability, a rule for a policy, a table for tangled logic, and never, ever forget the non-functional ones. You now have a rich set of requirements. But you cannot build them all at once, so next you will decide what comes first, and that is Module eight, where we prioritise and manage the whole set.
