Let us start with the job itself. A business analyst — and I will say BA from here on, because everyone in the trade does — works out what a business really needs, and why, and then describes, clearly, what any solution would have to do to meet that need. Notice what is not in that sentence. The BA is not necessarily the person who writes the software, runs the project, or signs the cheque. The BA is the person who makes sure all that effort is pointed at the right problem in the first place. Because the single most expensive mistake any organisation can make is to build the wrong thing, and build it beautifully. A good BA is the quiet insurance against exactly that.

You can think of a BA as a two-way translator. On one side sits the business — the managers, the warehouse, customer service, finance — who feel a problem keenly but describe it in frustrations and outcomes. On the other side sits delivery — the developers, the testers, the suppliers — who need precise, buildable detail before they can lift a finger. The BA stands in the gap and translates both ways: turning a vague ache into a clear statement of need, and turning that need into something a delivery team can actually act on. That is why so much of the work is listening, asking good questions, and writing things down plainly.

Now, a word about how this course is built, because it is a little unusual. Every module teaches from scratch. If you are completely new, you will be taken by the hand, with no jargon left unexplained, all the way to being job-ready. But every module then adds what we call a practitioner's corner — a deeper section for people who already do this work and want the subtler points, the common traps, and the things that separate a competent analyst from a senior one. So whether you are starting from zero, or you are already practising and want to go deeper, there is a lane for you. The techniques are mapped to the recognised industry standard, the business analysis body of knowledge, which you will hear called BABOK, so what you learn lines up with the wider profession rather than with any single exam.

To make all of this concrete, we follow one company through the whole course. Meet Homegrove, a mid-sized online retailer here in the United Kingdom, selling homeware and lifestyle goods — around forty-two million pounds of revenue a year, about three hundred staff, and something like a hundred and eighty thousand orders a year, through its own website and four physical shops, with a warehouse in Leicester and a head office in Manchester. And here is the important bit, said plainly, once: Homegrove is a fictional company we use for teaching. Every name, every figure, every twist in its story is invented to help the ideas land. Any resemblance to a real business is pure coincidence.

Homegrove's story begins with a single email. The chief operating officer writes to the technology team and says, more or less, customers keep complaining about late deliveries, so I think we need to rebuild the website — can we scope a new site? Hold on to that sentence, because it is the spark for the entire course. It is a solution handed down before anyone has understood the problem. And as you will discover, rebuilding the website might cost a small fortune and fix nothing at all. Learning to spot that trap, and to gently slow it down, is perhaps the most valuable instinct a BA can own.
