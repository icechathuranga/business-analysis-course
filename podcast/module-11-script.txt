Welcome back. Last time we got the integrated system funded and signed off. In this episode it has been built, it has gone live, and everyone at our fictional retailer, Homegrove, is quietly relieved it all worked on the day. And here is the sentence at the very centre of what follows: live is not the same as working. Going live just means the software runs. Whether it actually fixed anything, whether late deliveries fell, whether the worried phone calls dried up, whether the money the business was promised is genuinely showing up, that is a separate question. And answering it honestly is now your job.

The shorthands again. You are the business analyst, or BA. The thing that went live is the Order Management System, or OMS, the joined-up system connecting website, warehouse and couriers. This whole episode sits in an area of the body of knowledge called Solution Evaluation, which is really just a disciplined way of asking two questions: did we get the value we paid for, and if not, what should we do about it?

Let us start where delivery starts, with supporting the build and the testing. As the team builds, you keep clarifying requirements, you protect what they were meant to mean, and you run user acceptance testing, or UAT. That is where real users check that the built thing does what they actually need, not what a document said, but what they need. And here is the neat part: you already wrote the tests months ago. Every acceptance criterion you agreed alongside a requirement becomes a check. For Homegrove's live delivery-status feature, testing confirms the two things you agreed. That a dispatched order shows its courier status and estimated delivery date. And that when the status changes, the customer gets an email within fifteen minutes. One gentle warning: this testing exists to find problems while they are still cheap to fix, so never let it become a rubber stamp.

Now, measuring. You cannot tell whether a solution worked unless you decided, in advance, what good looks like, in numbers. These are your key performance indicators, or KPIs, and each one needs a baseline, a target, a source, and a rhythm for checking. At Homegrove the measures come straight out of the business case. Late deliveries: twelve per cent, heading for under four. Those where-is-my-order calls: twelve hundred a week, heading for under four hundred. Repeat-purchase rate: twenty-nine per cent, heading for thirty-three. And overselling, items sold that were not actually in stock: about twenty-five incidents a month, heading for under two. One subtlety worth knowing. Some of these move quickly and some move slowly. Deliveries and phone calls are leading indicators; they shift within weeks. Repeat-purchase is a lagging one; it takes months to confirm, because it depends on customers choosing to come back.

Then you analyse what the numbers are telling you. Imagine it is eight weeks after go-live. Suppose the late-delivery rate has dropped from twelve per cent to seven. That is real progress, but it is short of the under-four target. The amateur move is to celebrate, or to panic. The professional move is to ask why, and to segment. Is the lateness that is left in one courier lane? One product category? One time of day? Very often, when you break down the phone calls that remain, you find most of them are now about returns, rather than missing orders, which is a completely different problem to solve.

And here is the sharpest idea in the whole episode, the one that separates good evaluators from the rest. When a number misses its target, there are two very different reasons why, and they need opposite fixes. The first is a solution limitation, something inside the system itself. Perhaps the courier status feed refreshes less often than you expected, so the live status lags behind reality. You fix that in the system. The second is an enterprise limitation, something outside the system, in the people, the process, the training, the habits. And this is the one everybody underestimates. The system can be flawless and the numbers can still miss, because the warehouse team were never fully trained and are quietly keeping a private, just-in-case spreadsheet, the very re-keying the system was meant to kill. Remember Sam and his worry about job security. If his team has not truly adopted the new way of working, no software fix on earth will move that number. People change last, and slowest.

So when you come to recommend what to do next, follow the evidence, not the comfortable conversation. Logging a defect for the development team is easy. Saying we need to retrain the warehouse and retire that shadow spreadsheet is a harder conversation, and very often it is the true one. At Homegrove, with late deliveries stuck at seven per cent, the honest recommendation might well be both: a small fix to the courier feed, and a people-and-process change in the warehouse. Make one clear recommendation, give your reasoning, and name the risks that come with it.

One more discipline marks out the honest evaluator: be careful what you take credit for. If repeat-purchase does climb towards thirty-three per cent, can you really credit the new system, or did a marketing campaign, a price change, or just the season move it? Name those other possible causes rather than quietly claiming them all, because over-claiming benefits is the fastest way to lose the trust of finance on your next business case. And never confuse outputs with outcomes. The system processing a hundred and eighty thousand orders a year is activity; it is not the benefit. The benefit is fewer late deliveries, and fewer anxious customers reaching for the phone.

Two last ideas to leave you with. First, benefits take time. That two hundred and sixty thousand pounds of net annual benefit does not land on go-live day; it arrives over a full year. So you keep measuring, long after the project team has moved on, and you name an owner for those benefits. At Homegrove that is the sponsor, Priya, together with Marcus, who owns the process. Declaring victory on launch day, before the benefits have had any time to appear, is one of the most common mistakes even experienced analysts make. And second, once it has all bedded in, run a look back, a post-implementation review, capture what you learned, and feed those lessons into how you plan your next piece of work. Because evaluation is not the end of a line; it is the start of the next loop.

So, to carry away. Live is not working. Measure against a baseline you captured before the change, because a baseline you forgot to take is gone forever. Separate what is broken inside the system from what has simply not changed in the business. And have the honest conversation the numbers point to, because that is exactly how a project earns the right to be called a success.
