# Module 00 — What a Business Analyst really does

**One-line promise.** After this module you can explain, in plain words, what a business analyst (BA) does, where the role sits, how it differs from the roles around it, and how this whole course is laid out — so every later module has a place to hang.

**Where this sits.** This is orientation. It touches all six knowledge areas of the *Business Analysis Body of Knowledge* (BABOK v3) at a high level, rather than teaching any single technique. BABOK is published by the International Institute of Business Analysis (IIBA) and is the reference this course is aligned to.

**Why it matters.** Homegrove's Chief Operating Officer (COO) has just emailed IT: *"Customers keep complaining about late deliveries — I think we need to rebuild the website. Can we scope a new site?"* That one sentence is where this course begins, because it is a **solution handed down before anyone has understood the problem**. Rebuild the site and Homegrove could spend a fortune and fix nothing. The BA's job is to make sure the business solves the *right* problem — and that job is what this module introduces.

## What a business analyst actually does

A business analyst works out **what a business needs and why**, then defines **what any solution must do** to meet that need — clearly enough that the people who build it, test it, and pay for it all understand the same thing.

Notice what is *not* in that sentence: the BA does not have to be the person who builds the software, runs the project, or signs the cheque. The BA is the person who makes sure effort is pointed at the real problem and that everyone shares one, testable understanding of the goal. BAs deliver value by reducing the single most expensive risk in any change: **building the wrong thing well.**

Day to day that means talking to people, reading how the business really works, mapping processes, writing down needs as requirements, helping choose between options, and checking afterwards that the change actually helped. You will learn each of those as the course unfolds.

## Where the BA sits: between the business and delivery

Picture two groups who rarely speak the same language. On one side, the **business** — the COO, the warehouse, customer service, finance — who feel a problem but describe it in outcomes and frustrations. On the other, **delivery** — developers, testers, suppliers — who need precise, buildable detail.

The BA sits in the gap and translates both ways: turning a vague business ache into a clear statement of need, and turning that need into requirements delivery can act on. This "two-way translator" position is why BAs spend so much time listening, asking, and writing things down plainly.

## BA vs project manager vs product owner vs systems analyst

These roles overlap and are often confused. The quickest way to tell them apart is to ask which question each one *owns*.

| Role | The question they own | Primary focus |
|---|---|---|
| Business analyst (BA) | "What problem are we solving, and what must the solution do?" | Understanding the need; defining and managing requirements |
| Project manager (PM) | "How do we deliver this on time, on budget, with these people?" | Scope, schedule, cost, risk, delivery |
| Product owner (PO) | "What is the most valuable thing to build next?" | Owning and prioritising the product backlog (an agile role) |
| Systems analyst (SA) | "How will the system actually be built to do this?" | Technical and system design, closer to the build |

One person sometimes wears two hats (a BA/PO, say, on a small team), but the questions stay distinct. Confusing them is how projects end up with a careful schedule for building the wrong thing.

## The BA mindset: understand before you solve

If you remember one idea from this course, make it this: **understand the problem before you choose a solution.**

The COO's email already names a solution — "rebuild the website" — without stating the problem. A BA's instinct is to gently pause and ask: *what is actually going wrong for customers, and why?* That pause is not obstruction; it is the most valuable thing a BA does. You will meet this pattern — a solution handed down before the problem is framed — again and again, and Module 02 is built entirely around breaking it.

## The shape of the profession: BABOK v3 on one page

BABOK organises the work into **six knowledge areas**. You do not need to memorise them now — treat this as a map you will return to.

- **Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring** — deciding how you will do the BA work and with whom.
- **Elicitation and Collaboration** — drawing out needs from people and keeping them engaged.
- **Requirements Life Cycle Management** — tracing, prioritising, changing, and approving requirements over time.
- **Strategy Analysis** — understanding the current state, the future state, and the change between them.
- **Requirements Analysis and Design Definition (RADD)** — modelling, specifying, and validating requirements and design options.
- **Solution Evaluation** — checking that the delivered solution actually delivers value.

Around those six, BABOK adds **Underlying Competencies** — the personal skills a good BA relies on, such as analytical thinking, communication, interaction skills, and business knowledge — and five **Perspectives** that show how BA work flexes in different settings: Agile, Business Intelligence, Information Technology, Business Architecture, and Business Process Management. It also catalogues around **50 techniques** (interviews, process modelling, prioritisation, and so on) — the practical tools you will spend most of this course learning.

The IIBA offers certifications at different levels of experience — ECBA (entry), CCBA (mid-level), CBAP (senior), and the Agile Analysis certification (AAC). This course is written to be *aligned to the BABOK body of knowledge* rather than to coach a specific exam, so a beginner reaches job-ready and a practitioner can map everything back to the standard.

## Predictive vs adaptive: the BA in waterfall and agile

BABOK v3 describes two broad approaches to change. A **predictive** approach plans the work up front and delivers in larger, planned stages — often called "plan-first" or waterfall-style. An **adaptive** approach works in small increments and expects to learn and adjust as it goes — the agile, iterative style.

The BA mindset does not change between them; the *timing* and *size* of the work does. In a predictive project you might write a fuller set of requirements before build begins. In an adaptive one you do "just enough, just in time" analysis, feeding a prioritised backlog. You will go deep on this in Module 04 (planning) and Module 12 (agile BA). For now, just know both exist and a strong BA works comfortably in either.

## Meet Homegrove

Throughout the course you will analyse **Homegrove**, a mid-sized UK online retailer selling homeware and lifestyle goods — around £42M in annual revenue, 300 employees, and roughly 180,000 orders a year, through its own website and four physical shops, with a warehouse in Leicester and head office in Manchester. (Homegrove is fictional, invented purely for teaching.)

What the business can see so far are *symptoms*: late-delivery complaints up sharply, repeat custom slipping, and customer service swamped with "where is my order?" calls. What it has *not yet* done is find out why. That gap — between the symptoms everyone feels and the cause nobody has named — is exactly where your work as a BA begins in Module 02.

## Practitioner's corner (go deeper)

If you already work as a BA, the value of an orientation module is less the content than the *framing discipline* it sets up — so here are the nuances that separate a competent BA from a senior one.

First, **the six knowledge areas are not a sequence.** Beginners read them top to bottom and imagine a waterfall: plan, elicit, manage, analyse, build, evaluate. In reality the areas interleave continuously. You will be managing the requirements life cycle while still eliciting; you will revisit strategy analysis when a new constraint surfaces during design. BABOK is explicit that the areas describe *what* BA work involves, not a fixed order of operations. Treat the map as a checklist of concerns always in play, not a route march.

Second, **role boundaries are negotiated, not fixed.** The clean BA/PM/PO/SA table above is a teaching device. On real teams the lines blur, and the most common failure mode is not ignorance of the boundaries but silent assumption about who owns a decision. A senior BA names the ambiguity early — "who approves scope changes here, you or me?" — rather than discovering it during a dispute. The *Roles and Permissions Matrix* (you will meet it as a RACI chart — Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — in Module 03) exists precisely to make these lines explicit.

Third, **"understand before you solve" is a stance, not a phase.** It is easy to say and hard to hold when a powerful sponsor has already decided. The skill is doing it *without* appearing obstructive: you validate the sponsor's goal, then reframe their proposed solution as one option among several to be tested against the real need. That is diplomacy as much as analysis, and it is why Underlying Competencies sit alongside the technical knowledge areas rather than beneath them.

Common mistakes even senior BAs make:
- **Treating BABOK as a method to follow** rather than a body of knowledge to draw from. It describes the territory; it does not prescribe your route.
- **Equating "BA" with "requirements writer."** The highest-value BA work is upstream — framing the problem — long before a requirement is written.
- **Under-investing in the soft competencies** because they are harder to evidence than a tidy document.

## Key takeaways

- A BA works out **what the business needs and why**, then defines **what a solution must do** — reducing the risk of building the wrong thing.
- The BA is a **two-way translator** between the business and delivery.
- **BA, PM, PO, and SA** each own a different question; confusing them wastes effort.
- **Understand the problem before choosing a solution** — the spine of this whole course, and the trap in the COO's email.
- BABOK v3 has **six knowledge areas**, underlying competencies, five perspectives, and ~50 techniques; approaches are **predictive** or **adaptive**.
