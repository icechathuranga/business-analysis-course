# Module 10 — Communicating, presenting & sign-off

**One-line promise:** after this module you can tailor business analysis information to any audience, turn a dense business case into a one-page story and a confident ten-minute pitch, run a review that actually catches problems, handle conflict in the room, and walk out with a recorded sign-off.

**Where this sits:** day-to-day communication is the **Communicate Business Analysis Information** task in the **Elicitation & Collaboration (E&C)** knowledge area of the **BABOK® Guide** (Business Analysis Body of Knowledge). Formal **sign-off is different** — approval is the **Approve Requirements** task in **Requirements Life Cycle Management (RLCM)**, the governance gate you met in Module 08. The headline techniques here are **Reviews** and **Workshops**.

**Why it matters:** all your analysis is now worth exactly as much as you can make others understand and act on it. Homegrove's business case is sound — the integrated **Order Management System (OMS)** pays back in about 11 months with a 225% three-year return — but Priya — Chief Operating Officer (COO) and sponsor — originally asked IT to *rebuild the website*, and Nadia (Finance Business Partner) controls the money and signs off the spend. This is the moment the whole engagement turns on: present well and reframe gracefully, and the project is funded; present badly, and the best analysis in the building dies in a meeting.

## Core content

### Communicate business analysis information *(BABOK task: Communicate Business Analysis Information)*

**What it is.** Delivering the right information, to the right people, in the right form, at the right time — choosing package and channel to fit the audience's needs, not your convenience.

**When to use it.** Constantly, and deliberately before every significant touchpoint.

**How to do it.**
1. Identify the audience and what *decision or action* you need from them.
2. Pick the form — a one-pager for an executive, a detailed spec for a developer, a demo for an end user.
3. Choose the channel (written for a record, face-to-face for persuasion and nuance).
4. Pitch the depth and language to their world; define jargon or drop it.

**Homegrove.** For Priya you prepare a one-page readout and a short verbal pitch focused on complaints and cost; for Nadia, the financial detail and sensitivity; for Lee (IT), the integration and security view; for Sam (warehouse), a plain, reassuring note about redeployment, not redundancy. Same analysis, four packages.

**Pitfall.** One-size-fits-all communication — sending the 30-page business case to everyone. Executives won't read it; the people who need the detail get buried.

### Documentation vs visual communication *(industry)*

**What it is.** The deliberate choice between written artefacts (a record you can trace and approve) and visual ones (a diagram or picture that conveys a whole in seconds).

**When to use it.** Documentation when you need durability, traceability and sign-off; visuals when you need shared understanding fast, especially across functions.

**How to do it.** Lead with a visual to build understanding, then anchor decisions in documentation. A process map (Module 06) in the room, a baselined requirements document behind it.

**Homegrove.** The as-is swimlane showing the twice-daily re-keying lands the problem in one glance; the written business case and baselined Must/Should list (Module 08) carry the approval.

**Pitfall.** A beautiful diagram with no written decision behind it — or a 40-page document no one opens. You usually need both, each doing its own job.

### Storytelling with data and "project on a page" *(industry)*

**What it is.** Structuring information as a narrative — situation, complication, resolution, ask — and compressing a project onto a single page a busy stakeholder can absorb at a glance.

**When to use it.** For any executive or steering-committee communication, and whenever the data alone won't move people.

**How to do it.**
1. Use a simple arc: here's where we are (situation), here's what's going wrong (complication), here's the answer and what it's worth (resolution), here's what I need from you (the ask).
2. Make each number mean something human — not "1,200 contacts a week" but "1,200 anxious customers phoning every week to ask where their order is".
3. Put the whole thing on one page: problem, recommended option, cost, benefit, payback, key risks, and the decision needed.

**Homegrove.** The project-on-a-page opens with the complaint trend and the 1,200 weekly "where is my order?" contacts, names the root cause (manual hand-off, not the website), recommends the OMS, and states the headline numbers — one-off £240,000, net annual benefit £260,000, payback about 11 months, three-year net present value (NPV) about £406,600 — then the ask: approval to proceed. Build it from the **readout-one-pager.docx** template.

**Pitfall.** Drowning the story in data. One chart that makes the point beats six that invite debate. A page that needs explaining is not a one-pager.

### Presenting to a steering committee *(industry; applies Communicate Business Analysis Information)*

**What it is.** A short, structured, persuasive session that takes decision-makers from problem to approval — typically ten minutes of content and the rest discussion.

**When to use it.** At each major gate; here, to secure funding for the OMS.

**How to do it.**
1. **Open with the answer** (answer-first): "I'm recommending we fund an integrated OMS; it pays back in about 11 months."
2. Tell the story — situation, complication, resolution — then the numbers.
3. **Reframe, don't contradict.** Priya asked to rebuild the website; honour the instinct, then redirect with evidence: "You were right that customers are suffering — the analysis traced it to how orders reach the warehouse, not the storefront, so this fixes the cause your customers are feeling." She keeps her credibility; the decision changes.
4. **Pre-empt the finance questions.** Nadia will ask where the benefits come from, how firm they are, and when the cash returns — answer before she asks, and bring the sensitivity analysis.
5. Close on the explicit ask and the decision you need today.

**Homegrove.** You present to Priya and Nadia together: Priya buys the outcome (fewer complaints, controlled cost), Nadia buys the numbers (defensible benefits, about-11-month payback, positive NPV at the 10% hurdle rate).

**Pitfall.** Making the sponsor wrong in public. "Rebuilding the website would have wasted the money" is true and fatal — reframe around what she got right, never around her mistake.

### Reviews and walkthroughs *(BABOK technique: Reviews)*

**What it is.** A structured examination of a work product by others to find errors, confirm quality and build agreement. Reviews range from an informal desk-check or walkthrough (the author talks others through it) to a formal inspection.

**When to use it.** Before anything goes for approval. A requirement, a process model or a business case should be reviewed before it is presented, so the sign-off meeting confirms quality rather than discovering defects.

**How to do it.** Choose the formality to fit the stakes; circulate the material in advance; focus feedback on the product, not the person; log issues with owners (item tracking, Module 08); confirm they are resolved before approval.

**Homegrove.** You walk Marcus and Lee through the to-be process and the OMS requirements before the steering committee, so Marcus's "the returns workflow doesn't handle exchanges" surfaces in a review — fixable — not in front of Priya.

**Pitfall.** Reviewing to rubber-stamp. A review where no one is allowed to find anything wrong is a ceremony, and the defects simply surface later at higher cost.

### Workshops and facilitation *(BABOK technique: Workshops)*

**What it is.** A structured, facilitated session bringing stakeholders together to produce a defined output — agreed requirements, a prioritised list, a resolved conflict. Facilitation is the neutral skill of running it.

**When to use it.** When you need many voices aligned in one place and a decision by the end, not a month of email.

**How to do it.** Set a clear objective and agenda; invite the right people (decision-makers, not just delegates); establish ground rules; keep discussion on track and everyone contributing; capture decisions and actions live; close by confirming outcomes.

**Homegrove.** You ran prioritisation (Module 08) as a facilitated workshop so Marcus, Dana and Tom agreed the Must/Should list *together* — which is why no one relitigates it in the approval meeting. As facilitator you stay neutral on content and own the process.

**Pitfall.** The business analyst (BA) stops facilitating and starts advocating. The moment you argue for your own answer, you lose the neutrality that lets you broker everyone else's.

### Managing conflict and building consensus *(industry)*

**What it is.** Turning disagreement into a decision the group can live with — consensus meaning everyone can support it, not that everyone's first choice won.

**When to use it.** Whenever stakeholders pull in different directions — here, Sam's fear of job loss versus Marcus's drive for efficiency.

**How to do it.** Surface the real interest beneath each position ("Sam isn't against the OMS, he's afraid of redundancy"); find shared objectives; use objective criteria (the prioritisation scores, the business case) to depersonalise the choice; use a parking lot for off-topic issues; agree a decision rule in advance so you don't stall.

**Homegrove.** You defuse Sam's resistance by making redeployment explicit and involving him early (Module 03), so by approval day the warehouse is an ally, not a blocker.

**Pitfall.** Confusing consensus with unanimity, or papering over conflict to keep the peace — unspoken disagreement resurfaces as change requests and quiet non-adoption.

### Getting sign-off (Approve Requirements) *(BABOK task: Approve Requirements)*

**What it is.** The formal, recorded agreement by those with authority that the requirements and/or business case are correct and may proceed. In BABOK this is **Approve Requirements** in RLCM — approval is a governance gate, not a conversation, which is why it lives apart from the day-to-day Communicate task.

**When to use it.** At each baseline and for the business case. For Homegrove: Priya approves the scope and change as sponsor; Nadia approves the spend.

**How to do it.**
1. Confirm *who* has the authority to approve what (clarified back in Module 08).
2. Present the reviewed, baselined material.
3. Resolve any final conditions.
4. **Record** the approval — who approved, what, when, with what conditions — and re-baseline.

**Homegrove.** Priya and Nadia approve the OMS business case subject to a phased rollout and captured baseline metrics; you minute it and update the baseline. That record is what protects the scope against later drift and what you measure benefits against in Module 11.

**Pitfall.** Treating a nod in the room as sign-off. If it is not recorded, with the approver and the conditions named, you have not been approved — you have been encouraged.

## Practitioner's corner (go deeper)

**Communication is the BA's highest-leverage skill.** Elicitation finds the need and modelling makes it clear, but communication is where analysis becomes action — and it is the competency on which BAs are most often judged by senior stakeholders. The craft is *audience modelling*: before any significant message, a practitioner silently answers three questions — what does this person already believe, what decision do I need from them, and what is the one thing that will move them? Priya is moved by customer pain and cost control; Nadia by defensible numbers; Lee by security and integration risk. The content is constant; the framing is engineered per audience.

**Answer-first, always.** Executives read and listen top-down. Lead with the recommendation, support with evidence, and keep the detail in reserve for those who dig. The common failure is the chronological reveal — walking the committee through your whole journey before arriving at the point — which bores decision-makers and invites them to reach their own conclusion before you reach yours.

**Keep the governance line crisp.** This is the exam-grade distinction and a real-world one: **communicating** information is Elicitation & Collaboration, but **approving** it is Requirements Life Cycle Management, and **confirming elicitation results** is yet another task again (also E&C). Approval is a controlled gate with a named authority and a record; a walkthrough or a readout is collaboration. Blur them and you will mistake enthusiasm for authorisation — the fastest route to building something no one with budget actually signed off.

**Facilitation neutrality is a discipline, not a personality trait.** The hardest moment for a BA facilitator is when they know the answer and the room is heading elsewhere. The professional move is to make the *criteria* explicit and let the group apply them, not to seize the floor. If you must advocate, hand facilitation to someone else for that item — you cannot be both referee and player.

**Three mistakes even senior BAs make.** First, confusing *having communicated* with *being understood* — send-and-assume instead of checking comprehension and commitment. Second, winning the argument and losing the person — making a sponsor wrong in public may carry the decision today but costs you the relationship that funds the next project. Third, leaving the meeting on a verbal "yes" with nothing recorded; a practitioner always closes the loop in writing — who approved what, when, and under which conditions.

## Key takeaways

- **Tailor every message to its audience** — package, channel, depth and framing all change; the analysis does not.
- **Lead with the answer** and tell it as a story: situation, complication, resolution, ask.
- **Reframe, don't contradict** — honour Priya's instinct, then redirect with evidence to the real root cause.
- **Review before you present, and facilitate neutrally** so the approval meeting confirms quality rather than finding defects.
- **Communicating lives in Elicitation & Collaboration; sign-off is Approve Requirements in RLCM** — a recorded governance gate, not a nod.
- **If it isn't recorded, it isn't approved** — name the approver, the scope and the conditions, then re-baseline (ready for Module 11).
