# Module 13 — Capstone: run a BA engagement end to end

**One-line promise:** after this module you will have run a complete business analysis engagement — from a vague "rebuild the website" request to a measured benefits plan — and produced a portfolio-ready set of deliverables you can show an employer.

**Where this sits:** this capstone draws on all six knowledge areas of the **BABOK Guide** (the Business Analysis Body of Knowledge, maintained by the International Institute of Business Analysis, or IIBA): Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring; Elicitation and Collaboration; Requirements Life Cycle Management; Strategy Analysis; Requirements Analysis and Design Definition; and Solution Evaluation. It is where the whole toolkit becomes one continuous story.

**Why it matters:** across this course you have met each technique in isolation on the **Homegrove** case — from the COO's (Chief Operating Officer) "rebuild the website" email to the Order Management System (OMS) business case. A real engagement is one unbroken arc where each piece feeds the next: the problem you frame shapes who you talk to, who you talk to shapes what you elicit, what you elicit shapes your requirements, and so on to the measured result. Doing that whole arc once, end to end, is what turns knowledge into capability — and it gives you something most beginners lack: a concrete body of work to put in front of an interviewer.

## Core content

### Choose your context

Run this capstone on **Homegrove** (the running case — the OMS that replaces the twice-daily manual re-keying of orders) or on a real initiative from **your own workplace**. Homegrove gives you a complete, consistent data set; your own workplace gives you a portfolio piece that is unmistakably yours. Many learners do Homegrove first for practice, then repeat the arc on their own context.

### The engagement, phase by phase

Work through the phases in order. Each names the module to revisit and the downloadable template (in Word or Excel) to produce the deliverable. Do not skip phases — the point of the capstone is the join between them.

**Phase 0 — Orient (Module 00).** Re-read the BA role and mindset: *understand before you solve*. Write one paragraph on where you, the business analyst (BA), sit between the business and delivery for this engagement. *No template.*

**Phase 1 — Understand strategy and context (Module 01).** Produce a short strategy brief: the business need, drivers and desired future state, using a strategy tool such as the Business Model Canvas. *No template.*

**Phase 2 — Frame the problem and root cause (Module 02).** Write a sharp problem statement and trace the root cause — resisting the handed-down "rebuild the website" solution. Deliverable via **problem-root-cause.docx**.

**Phase 3 — Analyse stakeholders (Module 03).** Build a stakeholder register, a power/interest grid and a RACI matrix (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed). Deliverable via **stakeholder-raci.xlsx**.

**Phase 4 — Plan the BA work (Module 04).** Decide your approach — predictive, adaptive or hybrid — and sketch the BA work plan, governance and how you will measure your own performance. *No template.*

**Phase 5 — Plan and run elicitation (Module 05).** Produce an elicitation plan and an interview guide, choosing techniques to fit each stakeholder. Deliverable via **elicitation-plan.docx**.

**Phase 6 — Model the process and data (Module 06).** Draw the as-is and to-be process (showing where the manual hand-off disappears) and a simple order/stock data model. *No template.*

**Phase 7 — Specify requirements (Module 07).** Write up business, stakeholder, solution (functional and non-functional) and transition requirements, with user stories and acceptance criteria. Deliverable via **brd.docx** (the Business Requirements Document).

**Phase 8 — Prioritise and manage requirements (Module 08).** Build a MoSCoW-ordered backlog (Must, Should, Could, Won't-have-this-time), score items by value and effort, and set up traceability. Deliverables via **user-story-backlog.xlsx**, **prioritisation-matrix.xlsx** and **traceability-matrix.xlsx**.

**Phase 9 — Options, feasibility and business case (Module 09).** Compare design options and build the cost-benefit case — return on investment (ROI), payback and net present value (NPV). Deliverable via **business-case.xlsx**.

**Phase 10 — Communicate and get sign-off (Module 10).** Produce a stakeholder presentation and a one-page readout, and plan how you win approval. Deliverable via **readout-one-pager.docx**.

**Phase 11 — Plan evaluation and benefits (Module 11).** Define the performance measures, baselines and targets, name a benefits owner, and write a benefits-realisation and post-implementation-review plan. *No template.*

**Phase 12 — Decide how you'd deliver it (Module 12).** Show how the backlog would run on an agile team: a user story map, a Definition of Ready and a Definition of Done. *No template.*

### Self-assessment rubric

Rate each deliverable honestly against these levels. Aim for *Job-ready* across the board before you treat the set as portfolio-grade; *Strong* is where a practitioner is heading.

| Criterion | Not yet | Developing | Job-ready | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem framing | Jumps to a solution | States a problem, thin on root cause | Clear problem and evidenced root cause | Distinguishes symptom, problem, need and cause with data |
| Stakeholders | A flat name list | Grid or RACI present | Register, grid and RACI, engagement tailored | Resistance and influence actively managed |
| Elicitation | Ad hoc | Plan exists | Technique matched to each stakeholder | Results confirmed and triangulated |
| Modelling | None | One diagram | As-is and to-be plus data model, consistent | Models drive requirements and reveal gaps |
| Requirements | Vague wishes | Some testable items | Typed, testable, with acceptance criteria | Traceable to need and consistently well-formed |
| Prioritisation | Everything is a Must | MoSCoW applied | MoSCoW plus value/effort, defensible | Trade-offs explained to stakeholders |
| Business case | Costs only | ROI or payback | ROI, payback and NPV, assumptions stated | Risks and sensitivities tested |
| Communication | Document dump | Readable deck | Audience-tailored, clear recommendation | Wins the room and the sign-off |
| Evaluation | None | Measures named | Baselines, targets and owner set | Attribution and benefits tracking designed |

## Try it

Run the full arc on Homegrove: work Phases 0 to 12 in order, producing each deliverable from its named template, ticking the checklist as you go, and finishing with a self-assessment against the rubric. If time is short, run a *vertical slice* instead — take just the live-delivery-status need and carry it alone from problem statement, through one requirement with acceptance criteria, to one measure — so you still practise every join in miniature.

*Or do it for your own workplace:* repeat the arc on a real initiative you can speak about, swapping Homegrove's figures for your own and keeping any sensitive data out.

## Practitioner's corner (go deeper)

**This corner is about turning the capstone into a real portfolio, curriculum vitae (CV) and interview piece.**

A finished capstone is a career asset if you package it deliberately. Build a **portfolio** — a short case study of two or three pages, or a simple site — that tells the engagement as a story: the situation, what you were asked to do, what you actually did as the BA, and the outcome you designed for. Lead with the problem and the result, not the artefacts; then link the deliverables (the problem-root-cause worksheet, the stakeholder RACI, the BRD, the business case, the readout) as evidence beneath the narrative. Recruiters skim, so make the first paragraph carry the whole point.

On your **CV** and LinkedIn, translate the work into outcomes and competencies, not a list of documents. "Ran a self-study business analysis capstone that framed an order-management problem and built the investment case for a £240,000 system (225% three-year ROI)" says far more than "wrote a BRD". Name the techniques an employer screens for — stakeholder analysis, elicitation, process modelling, requirements, prioritisation, business case — because applicant-tracking systems and hiring managers both search for them.

For **interviews**, pre-load three or four stories in **STAR** form (Situation, Task, Action, Result) drawn straight from your capstone. The Homegrove arc gives you ready answers to the questions that always come up: "tell me about a time you challenged a stakeholder" (resisting the COO's "rebuild the website"), "how do you handle resistance" (the warehouse supervisor who fears job loss), "how do you prioritise" (MoSCoW and value/effort on the backlog), and "how do you know a project succeeded" (the four target measures and the benefits plan). Rehearse the *Action* and *Result* out loud — beginners under-tell what *they personally* did, which is exactly what interviewers probe.

Two cautions. First, **never expose real confidential data**: if you use your own workplace, anonymise names, figures and anything commercially sensitive, or use Homegrove as a safe stand-in. Second, **be honest about scope**: if the capstone was a learning exercise, say so — "a full engagement I ran as a self-study project" is credible and respected; dressing study work up as paid delivery is not, and experienced interviewers spot it instantly.

## Key takeaways

- The capstone joins every module (00 to 12) into one continuous engagement — the joins between phases are the real lesson.
- Work the phases in order; each produces a named deliverable from a downloadable template.
- Use the checklist to track completion and the rubric to judge quality honestly before calling the set portfolio-ready.
- Package the result as a story — situation, task, action, result — leading with problem and outcome, with the artefacts as evidence.
- Pre-load STAR interview answers from the arc, and always anonymise real workplace data.
