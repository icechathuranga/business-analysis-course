Module 01 — Strategy & business context

One-line promise. After this module you can read a business from the outside in — its model, environment, strengths, and strategy — and state the business need that any change must serve, before anyone argues about solutions.

Where this sits. This is Strategy Analysis in the Business Analysis Body of Knowledge (BABOK v3), published by the International Institute of Business Analysis (IIBA) — specifically the tasks Analyze Current State and Define Future State.

Why it matters. Homegrove's Chief Operating Officer (COO) wants to "rebuild the website" to stop late-delivery complaints. Before you can judge whether that helps, you have to understand what Homegrove is for, how it makes money, and where it is trying to go. A business analyst (BA) who skips the context ends up optimising a corner of a business that may be heading somewhere else entirely. This module gives you the lenses to see the whole board.

Business Model Canvas [BABOK]

What it is. A one-page picture of how an organisation creates, delivers, and captures value, laid out in nine linked building blocks (customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships, cost structure). It was popularised by Alexander Osterwalder.

When to use it. Early, to get a shared, whole-business view fast — especially when stakeholders each see only their own slice.

How to do it. Draw the nine blocks. Fill customer-facing blocks first (segments, value proposition, channels, relationships, revenue), then the engine behind them (resources, activities, partners), then the cost base. Look for strain: a block the proposed change would stress or strengthen.

Homegrove example. Segments: UK homeware shoppers, online and in four shops. Value proposition: good-value homeware, delivered conveniently. Channels: own website plus shops. Revenue: ~£42M a year, earned across both the website and the shops (~180,000 orders a year flow through the business). Key activities: fulfilment and delivery — exactly where the pain is being felt.

Pitfall. Treating it as a form to complete. The value is in the connections between blocks (a weak "delivery" activity poisons the "convenience" value proposition), not nine tidy lists.

VMOST / MOST [industry]

What it is. A top-to-bottom check that an organisation's Vision, Mission, Objectives, Strategy, and Tactics line up. MOST is the same tool without the Vision line. It is a widely-used industry framework, not a BABOK-named technique.

When to use it. When you need to test whether a proposed change actually serves the strategy — or expose that day-to-day tactics have drifted from stated goals.

How to do it. Write each level down, top first. Then read upward: does each tactic serve a strategy, each strategy an objective, and so on? Any orphan — a tactic serving no objective, or an objective with no strategy — is a flag.

Homegrove example. Vision: to be the homeware brand UK customers come back to. Mission: reliable, good-value homeware across web and shops. Objective: grow revenue by lifting customer retention. Strategy: make the buying-and-receiving experience dependable. Tactic (proposed): "rebuild the website" — which, read upward, does not obviously serve "dependable receiving." That mismatch is a clue.

Pitfall. Inventing crisp statements the business has never agreed. If a level is genuinely absent, record that gap rather than papering over it with your own wording.

SWOT Analysis [BABOK]

What it is. A four-box summary of internal Strengths and Weaknesses and external Opportunities and Threats. A BABOK technique for taking stock of current state.

When to use it. To consolidate what you have learned into something decision-makers grasp in seconds, and to connect internal capability to the outside world.

How to do it. Brainstorm each quadrant with evidence, not opinion. Then do the step most people skip: cross the quadrants — how can a strength seize an opportunity, or blunt a threat? Which weakness, left alone, lets a threat bite?

Homegrove example. Strengths: established brand, loyal base, four shops. Weaknesses: slipping delivery reliability; repeat custom down from 38% to 29%. Opportunities: retention-led growth; customers who value dependability. Threats: rivals with slicker fulfilment; rising customer expectations. The telling cross: a weakness (unreliable delivery) is eroding the strength (loyal base).

Pitfall. A SWOT that lists adjectives and stops. Without the crossing step and a resulting action, it is wallpaper.

PESTLE [industry]

What it is. An environmental scan of Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental forces acting on the business from outside. An industry framework that complements SWOT's external boxes; it is not a BABOK-named technique.

When to use it. When the change is big or long-lived enough that the outside world could shift under it, or when you suspect a "problem" is really an external trend.

How to do it. Take each letter in turn and ask: what in this category could affect our ability to deliver this change or realise its benefit? Keep only forces that are material and reasonably likely; note which you can influence and which you can only prepare for.

Homegrove example. Economic: squeezed household budgets make customers less forgiving of a late order. Social: shoppers now expect parcel tracking as standard. Technological: courier and retail systems routinely integrate by application programming interface (API). Legal: consumer-protection expectations around delivery and refunds (kept general here — confirm specifics with legal).

Pitfall. Listing generic macro-trends that never connect back to the decision at hand. Every entry should earn its place by changing an action.

Porter's Five Forces [industry]

What it is. A structured look at the competitive pressure in an industry across five forces — rivalry among existing competitors, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, and bargaining power of suppliers. Created by Michael Porter. It relates to the BABOK technique Benchmarking and Market Analysis, which formally compares your organisation against peers and market practice.

When to use it. When you need to understand why margins or loyalty are under pressure, or whether an advantage is defensible.

How to do it. Rate each force high/medium/low with evidence, then ask what the overall picture means for strategy. Pair it with Benchmarking and Market Analysis [BABOK] — comparing your delivery performance to competitors — to turn judgement into measured gaps.

Homegrove example. Buyer power: high — customers switch retailer easily after one bad delivery. Rivalry: high — many homeware sellers compete on convenience and speed. Substitutes: high street and marketplaces. The reading: in a market where buyers hold the power, reliability is a weapon, and losing it (falling retention) is strategically dangerous.

Pitfall. Using it on a single company rather than an industry. The five forces describe the arena, not one competitor.

Balanced Scorecard [BABOK]

What it is. A way to track performance across four balanced perspectives — Financial, Customer, Internal Process, and Learning and Growth — so an organisation does not chase short-term money while starving the things that sustain it. A BABOK technique.

When to use it. When you need to connect a strategic goal to the measures that show whether it is being met — and to show how an operational fix (process) feeds a financial result.

How to do it. For each perspective, name the objective, one or two measures, and a target. Trace the cause-and-effect upward: better learning enables better process, which improves the customer experience, which drives financial results.

Homegrove example. Customer: repeat-purchase rate (currently 29%). Internal process: on-time, accurate fulfilment. Financial: revenue retained from loyal customers. Laid out this way, a process problem (fulfilment) is visibly the lever behind a customer and financial outcome — not a backroom detail.

Pitfall. Drowning in metrics. A scorecard with thirty measures measures nothing; pick the few that genuinely signal the strategy.

From context to a business need, drivers, and a future state

All six lenses feed three linked outputs. The first is a clear business need — the problem or opportunity, stated at the level of the business, that any change must address. For Homegrove the context points consistently to one place: the business is pursuing growth through retention, and something about how customers receive their orders is undermining it. That is the need — and notice it still says nothing about a website or a system.

The second output is the drivers: the forces that make change worth doing now. Drivers are the pressures already bearing on the business — here, the rising late-delivery complaints, the slipping repeat-purchase rate, and the mounting customer-service load. Naming them turns "it would be nice to improve" into "here is why we must act."

The third is the desired future state, described as outcomes and capabilities, never as a chosen solution. For Homegrove the future state is simply: customers receive their orders on time and in full, can see where an order is, and are never sold something that isn't in stock. No platform is named; that restraint is deliberate.

Module 02 is where you turn this business-level need into a sharp, evidence-backed problem, and Module 09 is where the solution — whatever it turns out to be — earns its business case. You carry the strategic objective, lift retention, forward through both.

The techniques in this module

  • Business Model Canvas [BABOK] — whole-business value model on one page.
  • VMOST / MOST [industry] — vision-to-tactics alignment check.
  • SWOT Analysis [BABOK] — internal/external stock-take.
  • PESTLE [industry] — external environmental scan.
  • Porter's Five Forces [industry] — competitive-pressure analysis, paired with Benchmarking and Market Analysis [BABOK].
  • Balanced Scorecard [BABOK] — performance across four linked perspectives.

Try it

Using only the facts in this module, sketch a quick SWOT for Homegrove (four boxes, two or three evidenced points each). Then write one sentence crossing a weakness with a threat. Finally, state Homegrove's business need in a single sentence — with no solution named.

Or do it for your own workplace. Fill a Business Model Canvas for your own organisation from memory, then mark the one block your current project most affects. Does the project serve the strategy, or just a corner of it?

Practitioner's corner (go deeper)

Experienced BAs know these frameworks are cheap to run and easy to run badly. The difference is in sequencing, evidence, and knowing which lens answers which question.

Sequence them deliberately. A common misuse is running every framework as a ritual and producing a thick pack nobody reads. Choose by question: use the Business Model Canvas to see the whole; PESTLE and Porter's Five Forces for the outside world; SWOT to synthesise internal and external into decisions; VMOST to test alignment; the Balanced Scorecard to connect strategy to measures. In practice a tight Canvas plus a crossed SWOT plus a VMOST check often does more than all six half-done.

Current state and future state are different tasks. BABOK separates Analyze Current State from Define Future State for good reason. Most of the tools above describe where you are. The discipline is to resist leaping to the future state until the current state is evidenced — and, when you do define the future, to describe it as capabilities and outcomes ("accurate, on-time fulfilment with visibility for the customer"), not as a named system. Jumping straight to "the future state is a new platform" is strategy analysis collapsing back into solutioneering.

Strategy analysis is where you earn the right to reframe a sponsor's solution. When you can show the COO that the stated vision is retention, that buyer power is high, and that a crossed SWOT puts delivery reliability at the centre, "rebuild the website" becomes testable rather than given. That is the political value of doing this work before Module 02, not after.

Common mistakes even senior BAs make:

  • Confusing a goal with a strategy. "Grow retention" is an objective; "make receiving dependable" is the strategy to reach it. VMOST exists to catch exactly this slippage.
  • Running SWOT as a list, never crossing the quadrants — so it informs no decision.
  • Letting PESTLE drift into trivia — macro-trends with no link to the change burn credibility.

Key takeaways

  • Strategy Analysis means understanding the business, its environment, and its direction before judging any solution.
  • The Business Model Canvas, SWOT, and Balanced Scorecard are BABOK techniques; PESTLE, VMOST, and Porter's Five Forces are widely-used industry frameworks that complement them.
  • Frameworks earn their keep only when they change a decision — cross the SWOT, read VMOST upward, connect the scorecard to strategy.
  • The output of this module is a business need stated with no solution named — Homegrove's is growth through retention, undermined by how orders are received.
  • Keep Analyze Current State and Define Future State distinct, and describe the future as capabilities, not a chosen system.
